# THE CORNER HOUSE — 4부 60화

## Christmas Eve (크리스마스이브)

> **표기 전용 정보 (낭독하지 않음)**
> - 부/화: 4부 6화(통산 60화) · 시기: 2026년 12월 24일(목) 오후~밤 · 난이도: B2~C1 · 턴 수: 120
> - 장소: 2A 토미의 부엌 → 2A 거실의 긴 식탁 → 건물 앞 계단(스툽)
> - 등장인물: TOMMY, GINA, LUCA, AUNT CONNIE, FRANKIE, EDDIE
> - 사건: 앤젤라가 세상을 떠난 2019년 이후 토미는 '일곱 가지 생선' 만찬을 한 번도 차리지 않았다. 올해 지나가 엄마의 손글씨 레시피 카드를 들고 와서 다시 하자고 한다. 바칼라는 사흘 동안 물에 담가 뒀다. 밤에는 토미의 고모 코니(84)와 사촌 프랭키, 에디가 식탁에 앉는다. 프랭키는 엄마에게 베팅 빚을 털어놓는다. 자정 무렵 스툽에서 토미와 지나가 오래 묵은 이야기를 꺼낸다. 토미가 놓친 딸의 학예회들, 그리고 서로에게 주는 선물.
> - 학습 포인트: 가족 화법과 화해 표현(I owe you an apology. / Let's put it behind us. / I should have been there. / You did your best.), 과거 습관 회상(She used to… / Every year, we would…), 이탈리아계 음식 이름 발음
> - 미국 현안: 이탈리아계 미국인의 명절 전통, 오르는 해산물값, 스포츠 베팅 빚과 가족, 혼자 보내는 노인의 명절
> - 연결: 47화 앤젤라 이야기와 49화 '비밀 금지' 냅킨. 42화 프랭키의 베팅과 '엄마한텐 말하지 마'. 26화 토미 집안의 1910년 나폴리→컬럼비아 스트리트 이주. 53화 지미의 갈고리. 54화 '갈고리는 놓지 않는다'.

## 대본

[Scene: Thursday, 3:00 p.m. Apartment 2A. A small kitchen full of steam. A bowl of salt cod soaks in the sink. Gina spreads old index cards on the counter. Luca, four, stands on a chair in a dinosaur apron.]

GINA: Okay, there are seven cards for seven fishes, all in Mom's handwriting. Look, Dad, she even wrote "Tommy, DON'T touch" on the calamari one.
TOMMY: She wrote that in two thousand three, because I burned the calamari once. Once! And she never let me forget it.
GINA: Then don't touch the calamari. You're on the baccalà, which has been soaking since Monday.
TOMMY: That's three days of changing the water twice a day, like it's a baby. A very salty baby.
LUCA: Nonno, why is the fish taking a bath?
TOMMY: Because it's salty, buddy, very, very salty. The water takes the salt out, but it does it slowly.
LUCA: Does the fish like the bath?
TOMMY: The fish doesn't like anything anymore, buddy. The fish is retired.
GINA: Dad, please don't traumatize him. Luca, the fish is just sleeping, okay?
LUCA: Okay. Goodnight, fish, and sleep tight in your bath.
TOMMY: Gina, can I say something before we start?
GINA: Of course you can, Dad. What is it?
TOMMY: I didn't want to do this. When you called last week, I almost said no again.
GINA: I know. You said no in twenty twenty, and in twenty twenty-one, and every year since then.
TOMMY: Because it was her night. Your mother used to start cooking on the twenty-first, three whole days of it. She used to sing. It was terrible singing, all Dean Martin, and she got every song wrong.
GINA: "That's Amore." She sang it to the clams every single year, and they never complained.
TOMMY: And every year, we would fight about the eel. Your grandmother wanted eel, your mother hated eel, and I just wanted to watch the game.
GINA: And every year, you lost the fight, if I remember correctly.
TOMMY: Every year, and I lost it happily. After she died, I couldn't come into this kitchen on Christmas Eve, because it was too quiet.
GINA: That's why I'm here, to make it loud again. And I brought a four-year-old, who is very loud.
LUCA: I'm VERY loud!
TOMMY: You are. You're the loudest Russo since your great-grandfather, who sold fish on Columbia Street in nineteen twenty. He yelled his prices so loud that people heard him in New Jersey.
GINA: Okay, here's the menu. Fish one is baccalà, and fish two is fried calamari. Three is shrimp scampi, four is linguine with clams, five is fried smelts, six is octopus salad, and seven is stuffed clams.
TOMMY: No eel? Not even a little piece, for old times' sake?
GINA: No eel. Mom won that fight permanently, and I'm not reopening the case.
TOMMY: Good, because eel is forty dollars a pound now. Everything's crazy. The shrimp was twenty-two, and the clams were eighteen a dozen.
GINA: I know, because I paid. Don't look at the receipt. It's my Christmas present to you, and to Mom.
TOMMY: Gina... you don't have to pay for all this. You make less money now.
GINA: I make less money, but I have more time. That's the trade, and tonight, the time is for this.
LUCA: Nonno, can I put the flour on the squid?
TOMMY: Yes, but gently, like snow falling, not like a snowball.
LUCA: Like snow! Look, Nonno, it's snowing!
GINA: Dad, he's completely covered in flour. Even his eyebrows are white.
LUCA: Nonno, did Nonna like fish?
TOMMY: Nonna loved fish, but she loved clams the most. She called them "little treasure boxes."
LUCA: Treasure boxes! Can I open one?
TOMMY: Later, with a grown-up, because clams fight back.

[Scene: 7:30 p.m. The living room of 2A. Two tables pushed together, covered with a red cloth. Candles. Aunt Connie, eighty-four, sits at the head of the table in a fur hat she refuses to take off. Frankie sits next to her. Eddie sits across.]

AUNT CONNIE: Tommy, come here and let me look at you. You got fat.
TOMMY: Merry Christmas to you too, Zia Connie. It's nice to see you, too.
AUNT CONNIE: It's a compliment. Skinny men can't be trusted. Your uncle was skinny, and he was a crook.
FRANKIE: Ma! That was Dad you're talking about!
AUNT CONNIE: I know who it was. I was married to him for fifty-one years, so I'm allowed.
EDDIE: Mrs. Russo, I'm Eddie O'Neill, from the third floor. Thank you for letting an Irishman sit at an Italian table.
AUNT CONNIE: An Irishman at the Feast of the Seven Fishes. Are you at least Catholic?
EDDIE: Since nineteen forty-three. I go to Visitation Church every Sunday, or almost every Sunday.
AUNT CONNIE: Then you're half Italian already. Sit down and eat, because you're too thin too.
GINA: Okay, everybody, fish number one is the baccalà. Dad made it, and it took three days of work.
AUNT CONNIE: Let me taste. Hm. Hmm. It's good. It's not Angela's, but it's good.
TOMMY: Thank you, Zia. That's the nicest thing you've ever said to me, and I've known you for fifty-eight years.
AUNT CONNIE: Don't get used to it. Angela was a better cook than all of us, God rest her soul. She used to make me take home three containers.
EDDIE: She made me take home four, every Christmas. I lived on her scampi until January sixth.
GINA: She did? I never knew that, not in all those years.
EDDIE: She'd knock on my door on December twenty-sixth and say, "Eddie, you're alone. Take this." I never said no, because nobody said no to Angela.
FRANKIE: Can I... can I say something? Before the calamari comes out?
AUNT CONNIE: Before the calamari? Then it must be serious, because nobody interrupts calamari.
FRANKIE: Ma, I have to tell you something. I lost money, a lot of money, betting on my phone.
AUNT CONNIE: Betting? Like your uncle Vinny, with the horses?
FRANKIE: It's worse, because the phone is faster than horses. I lost about six thousand dollars this year. Tommy knows, and he told me to stop, so I deleted the app in October.
AUNT CONNIE: Six thousand dollars? Francesco, put down your fork and look at me.
FRANKIE: I'm sorry, Ma. I didn't want you to know, because I was ashamed.
AUNT CONNIE: Then why are you telling me now? On Christmas Eve, in front of the Irishman?
FRANKIE: Because Gina has a rule with Tommy: "No secrets." It's written on a napkin on his fridge, and I saw it when I came in.
TOMMY: It's true. It's right there, next to the Mets schedule, where I see it every morning.
AUNT CONNIE: Hmph. And are you still betting? Tell me the truth, on your father's memory.
FRANKIE: No. It's been two months without a single bet. I go to a meeting on Tuesdays, in a church basement in Bay Ridge.
AUNT CONNIE: A meeting. Good. Your father never went to a meeting. He went to the track instead.
FRANKIE: So you're not angry with me?
AUNT CONNIE: I'm furious. And I'm proud. Both. An Italian mother can be both at the same time, because it's our special talent.
EDDIE: Irish mothers too. My mother was furious and proud for forty years, but mostly furious.
AUNT CONNIE: Francesco, come here and give me a kiss. And next time, you tell me first, not after. I'm eighty-four, and I don't have time for "after."
FRANKIE: Ma, I owe you an apology. It's a big one, and I know it.
AUNT CONNIE: You owe me six thousand dollars of cannoli. But we'll put it behind us. Now pass the calamari before it gets cold.
TOMMY: Okay, here's the calamari. Gina made it, and I didn't touch it. I swear on Angela's card.
GINA: He really didn't, because I watched him the whole time. Luca did the flour.
AUNT CONNIE: The little one helped? Then it's perfect. Mangia! Everybody, mangia!
EDDIE: Tommy, tell your aunt about the building, and the co-op.
AUNT CONNIE: What co-op? What are you buying? You can't even buy a good shirt.
TOMMY: Maybe the building, Zia, all of us together, if the landlord sells.
AUNT CONNIE: Your grandfather rented on Columbia Street for forty years, and he never owned a brick. If you buy this building, I'll light a candle at Saint Rosalia's, a big one.
FRANKIE: She means it, too. Ma's candles are famous. The church almost caught fire in nineteen ninety.

[Scene: 11:45 p.m. The stoop outside the building. Cold. Christmas lights blink in Ahmed's store window across the street. Luca sleeps inside on Tommy's couch. Tommy and Gina sit on the top step with two small glasses of anisette.]

GINA: Zia Connie took five containers home. Five! And Eddie took four.
TOMMY: That's just what your mother used to do. Everybody leaves with food, and nobody leaves hungry. That's the rule.
GINA: Dad, can I tell you something? Since we're doing no secrets now.
TOMMY: Uh-oh. Is this another secret? Because I've had enough of those for one night.
GINA: It's not a secret. It's more like a feeling, and an old one. When I was a kid, I used to be angry at the bar.
TOMMY: At the bar? Why would you be angry at the bar?
GINA: Because you were always there. You missed my school plays, my recitals, and my soccer games. Mom came, but you were pouring beer.
TOMMY: Gina...
GINA: I was Mary in the Christmas pageant in third grade, and I had one line: "There's no room at the inn." I looked for you, but you weren't there.
TOMMY: I remember that night. It was a Friday, and Ramon called in sick, so I couldn't close.
GINA: I know that now, but I didn't know it then. I just knew that the seat next to Mom was empty.
TOMMY: I should have been there. I should have closed the bar, just for one Friday. I'm sorry, sweetheart. That apology is twenty-five years late, but I owe it to you.
GINA: You did your best. I know you did, because you were keeping us alive: the rent, the school, and later, Mom's medicine.
TOMMY: But you were eight, and an eight-year-old doesn't care about rent. She cares about the empty seat.
GINA: Okay. Thank you for saying that. I didn't know I needed to hear it. But I really did, Dad.
TOMMY: Let's put it behind us. We don't have to forget it, okay? We'll just put it behind us, like the eel.
GINA: Like the eel. Okay. Now I have a present for you, so open it.
TOMMY: What is this, a frame? It's... it's her card, the calamari card. "Tommy, DON'T touch."
GINA: I framed it for the bar. You can hang it behind the register, so she's always telling you what to do.
TOMMY: So she'll be bossing me around forever. That's perfect, Gina. Thank you.
GINA: Dad, you're crying again.
TOMMY: It's the anisette, because it's strong. Okay, my turn. Here, it's something small.
GINA: An envelope? Dad, what's this?
TOMMY: Open it. It's your mother's apron, the one with the lemons. No, wait. The apron is in the bag. The envelope is something else.
GINA: Twenty-five hundred dollars? In cash? Dad, what—
TOMMY: It's for your share. Not for my share, but for yours, in the co-op, if we win.
GINA: Dad, I don't live in the building, so I can't buy a share.
TOMMY: Not now, but maybe someday, in 4C or 3B, for you and Luca. Your mother always said, "Gina should live close." I'm saving it for that.
GINA: Dad, you have four hundred dollars in your checking account. I've seen your statements.
TOMMY: I had four hundred. Then I sold the jukebox to a guy in Bushwick for twenty-six hundred dollars, because he wanted it for his record store.
GINA: You sold the jukebox? The jukebox you said you regretted buying?
TOMMY: Small regrets. Big decisions. You taught me that, remember?
GINA: But what will you play at the bar now, without a jukebox?
TOMMY: Eddie has a radio, and it plays Irish music on Sundays. Dolores likes it.
GINA: Since when do you know what Dolores likes, Dad?
TOMMY: Since she started knocking on Eddie's floor. In this building, everybody knows everything.
GINA: Merry Christmas, Dad.
TOMMY: Merry Christmas, Gina. Your mother would have loved tonight: every fish, every container, even the flour on the kid.
GINA: She'd say the baccalà needed more lemon, though.
TOMMY: And she'd be right, because she was always right. Come on, it's midnight. Let's go get the kid.

## 표현 정리 (낭독하지 않음)

- **화해 표현**
  - *I owe you an apology.* — "너한테 사과할 게 있어." 진지한 사과의 시작.
  - *I should have been there.* — "내가 거기 있었어야 했는데." 과거에 대한 후회.
  - *You did your best.* — "넌 최선을 다했어." 상대의 사과를 받아 주는 말.
  - *Let's put it behind us.* — "이제 지난 일로 하자." 잊자는 게 아니라 넘어가자는 것.
  - *I didn't know I needed to hear it.* — "그 말이 필요했는지 몰랐어."
- **과거 습관 회상**
  - *She used to sing. / She used to make me take home three containers.* — used to + 동사원형.
  - *Every year, we would fight about the eel.* — would는 과거에 반복된 행동을 이야기처럼 들려준다.
- **이탈리아계 음식 이름**
  - *baccalà* 바칼라(소금에 절인 대구) [바-카-**라**]
  - *calamari* 칼라마리(오징어 튀김) [칼-러-**마**-리]
  - *scampi* 스캄피(마늘 버터 새우) [**스캠**-피]
  - *smelts* 빙어류 작은 생선 / *octopus salad* 문어 샐러드 / *stuffed clams* 속을 채운 조개
  - *anisette* 아니스 향 리큐어 [애-니-**셋**]
- **Zia** — 이탈리아어로 고모·이모. *Mangia!* — "먹어!"
- **Feast of the Seven Fishes** — 이탈리아계 미국인의 크리스마스이브 해산물 만찬.
- **Skinny men can't be trusted.** — 코니 고모식 과장. 칭찬인지 욕인지 모르게 하는 노인의 말투.
- **I'm furious. And I'm proud. Both.** — 상반된 감정을 함께 말하는 표현.

## 낭독 포인트 (낭독하지 않음)

- 음식 이름은 강세 음절을 분명하게. 특히 *baccalà*는 마지막 음절에 강세.
- 코니 고모는 느리고 단정적으로, 문장 끝을 확 내린다: *You got fat.↘ / It's a compliment.↘*
- 프랭키의 고백은 머뭇거리며: *Can I... can I say something?* — 말이 끊긴다.
- 마지막 장면은 추운 밤의 속삭임. *There's no room at the inn.* — 지나가 여덟 살의 자신을 떠올리며 조금 아이처럼.
- 토미의 *I should have been there.*는 *should have*를 줄이지 말고 천천히, 또박또박.
- *Small regrets. | Big decisions.* — 52화의 문장을 다시 꺼낸다. 미소 짓듯이.

## 한글 해석

지나: 좋아요, 생선 일곱 가지에 카드 일곱 장, 전부 엄마 글씨예요. 봐요, 아빠, 칼라마리 카드엔 "토미, 손대지 마"라고까지 써 놨어요.
토미: 그거 2003년에 쓴 거야. 내가 칼라마리를 한 번 태웠거든. 딱 한 번! 근데 평생 그 얘기를 하더라.
지나: 그럼 칼라마리는 손대지 마요. 아빠는 월요일부터 담가 둔 바칼라 담당이에요.
토미: 사흘 동안 하루에 두 번씩 물을 갈아 줬어. 아기처럼. 아주 짠 아기.
루카: 논노, 생선이 왜 목욕을 해요?
토미: 짜서 그래, 꼬마야. 아주, 아주 짜거든. 물이 소금을 빼 주는데, 천천히 빼.
루카: 생선은 목욕 좋아해요?
토미: 생선은 이제 아무것도 안 좋아해, 꼬마야. 생선은 은퇴했어.
지나: 아빠, 제발 애한테 트라우마 주지 마요. 루카, 생선은 그냥 자고 있는 거야, 알았지?
루카: 알았어요. 잘 자, 생선. 목욕하면서 푹 자.
토미: 지나, 시작하기 전에 하나 말해도 돼?
지나: 물론이죠, 아빠. 뭔데요?
토미: 사실 하기 싫었어. 지난주에 네가 전화했을 때, 또 싫다고 할 뻔했어.
지나: 알아요. 2020년에도 싫다고 했고, 2021년에도, 그 뒤로 매년 그랬잖아요.
토미: 그건 엄마의 밤이었으니까. 네 엄마는 21일부터 요리를 시작하곤 했어. 꼬박 사흘을. 노래도 불렀어. 끔찍한 노래였지. 전부 딘 마틴인데, 가사는 매번 틀리고.
지나: "That's Amore." 매년 조개한테 불러 줬어요. 조개들은 한 번도 불평 안 했고요.
토미: 그리고 매년 우린 장어 때문에 싸웠지. 네 할머니는 장어를 원했고, 네 엄마는 장어를 싫어했고, 난 그냥 경기를 보고 싶었고.
지나: 그리고 매년 아빠가 졌죠. 내 기억이 맞다면요.
토미: 매년. 그것도 기꺼이. 엄마가 죽고 나서는 크리스마스이브에 이 부엌에 못 들어오겠더라. 너무 조용해서.
지나: 그래서 내가 온 거예요. 다시 시끄럽게 하려고요. 그리고 네 살짜리도 데려왔어요. 얘 엄청 시끄러워요.
루카: 나 엄청 시끄러워요!
토미: 그렇지. 넌 네 증조할아버지 이후로 제일 시끄러운 루소야. 그분은 1920년에 컬럼비아 스트리트에서 생선을 팔았어. 값을 어찌나 크게 외쳤는지 뉴저지에서도 들렸대.
지나: 좋아요, 메뉴예요. 생선 하나는 바칼라, 둘은 칼라마리 튀김. 셋은 새우 스캄피, 넷은 조개 링귀니, 다섯은 작은 생선 튀김, 여섯은 문어 샐러드, 일곱은 속 채운 조개.
토미: 장어는 없어? 옛날 생각해서 한 조각도?
지나: 장어 없어요. 그건 엄마가 영원히 이겼고, 다시 따질 생각 없어요.
토미: 잘됐다. 장어가 요즘 파운드당 40달러거든. 다 미쳤어. 새우는 22달러였고, 조개는 열두 개에 18달러였어.
지나: 알아요. 내가 냈으니까요. 영수증 보지 마요. 아빠한테, 그리고 엄마한테 주는 크리스마스 선물이에요.
토미: 지나… 이걸 다 네가 낼 필요 없어. 너 이제 돈 덜 벌잖아.
