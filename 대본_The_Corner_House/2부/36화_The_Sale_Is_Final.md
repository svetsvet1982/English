## 대본

[Scene: Friday, 11:30 a.m. The front hall. Mrs. Esposito and Eddie each hold a thick envelope.]

MRS. ESPOSITO: Eddie. Eddie, don't open it yet. Wait for Marisol, because she understands these letters better than we do.
EDDIE: Another white envelope. I'm starting to hate the mailman, and he's a very nice young man.
MRS. ESPOSITO: It's not the mailman's fault. It's a certified letter, so I had to sign for it at the door.
EDDIE: Me too. I signed my name with a shaking hand, and it didn't even look like my name.
MARISOL: I'm here, and I got one too. Everybody in the building got one. Okay, let's open them together.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Together, just like we did with the lease renewals.
EDDIE: The lease renewals were good news, though. This doesn't feel like good news to me.
MARISOL: Okay, ready? One, two, three.
MRS. ESPOSITO: "Notice of Change of Ownership." Oh no.
EDDIE: Read it, Marisol. My eyes don't want to, and my hands won't stop shaking.
MARISOL: "Dear Resident. Please be advised that the property at the corner of Van Brunt and Pioneer has been sold."
MRS. ESPOSITO: Sold. It's real. It's really real. We knew it was coming, but it still feels like a punch.
MARISOL: "The new owner is Harborline Red Hook LLC, an affiliate of Harborline Capital. The sale closed on August twenty-fourth."
EDDIE: August twenty-fourth was Monday. They sold our home on Monday, and nobody told us until today.
MARISOL: "Effective September first, all rent payments must be made to the new managing agent. Please see the enclosed instructions."
MRS. ESPOSITO: The managing agent? Who's that? Is it a person or a company?
MARISOL: It's a company called "Bridgewater Property Management." They have an address in Manhattan, a website, and an app.
EDDIE: An app! You mean I have to pay my rent with an app now?
MARISOL: No, you can still pay by check, because it says so right here. "Checks may be mailed to the following address."
EDDIE: Thank God. I like checks, because checks are real and you can hold them.
MRS. ESPOSITO: What else does it say? Keep going, Marisol.
MARISOL: "All existing leases will remain in full force and effect."
EDDIE: What does that mean? Tell me in English, please.
MARISOL: It means your lease is still good, so your rent doesn't change. At least, it doesn't change because of the sale.
EDDIE: Okay. Okay. That's something.
MARISOL: Then there's this part. "We are excited to begin a program of capital improvements to the building."
MRS. ESPOSITO: Capital improvements. They sound excited, but what does that actually mean?
MARISOL: It means renovations, and big ones. Maybe the roof, the boiler, and the halls, or maybe the empty apartment.
EDDIE: Renovations. That sounds nice, actually. A new boiler would be very nice in January.
MARISOL: It can be nice. Or it can be noise, dust, and scaffolding for a whole year. Remember what the lawyer told us.
MRS. ESPOSITO: First they make life uncomfortable, and then they offer you money to leave.
EDDIE: Well, nobody is offering me money. And even if they do, I'm not leaving.
MARISOL: And here's the last part. "We look forward to meeting our residents. Sincerely, Grant Whitlock, Managing Director."
MRS. ESPOSITO: Grant Whitlock. Is that the man in the suit, the one with the camera?
MARISOL: I think so. Before, he was just a suit with a camera, but now he has a name.

[Scene: 1:00 p.m. The Hook. Tommy holds two envelopes. Tasha, Jesse, and Marisol around him.]

TOMMY: Two letters. I got two letters, one for the apartment and one for the bar.
TASHA: Read the bar one first. Come on, Tommy. I can't stand it.
TOMMY: "Dear Commercial Tenant. As the new owner, we have reviewed your lease. Your lease expires May thirty-first, twenty twenty-seven."
JESSE: Well, that's what you already thought. It's May of next year.
TOMMY: "We will contact you in due course regarding renewal terms, which will reflect current market conditions."
TASHA: "Current market conditions." What does that mean for the bar?
MARISOL: It means the rent will go up to whatever the market says, and that's probably a lot.
TOMMY: And "in due course"? What's "in due course" supposed to mean?
JESSE: It just means "later," whenever they want. It's corporate language, so it sounds polite but says nothing.
TOMMY: So they'll call me later, when they feel like it. And then they'll double my rent.
MARISOL: We don't know that yet. But it's possible, because commercial rents on the water have gone up a lot.
TASHA: So how much is a space like this, right on the water, in twenty twenty-six?
JESSE: I can check on my phone. Give me a second... There's a restaurant space two blocks away, about the same size. It's fourteen thousand a month.
TOMMY: Fourteen thousand! I pay sixty-five hundred!
TASHA: That's more than double what you pay now!
TOMMY: I can't pay fourteen thousand. Nobody can pay fourteen thousand a month selling wings and eight-dollar beer.
MARISOL: Tommy, breathe. It's just a listing, so it's not an offer. Not yet.
TOMMY: "Not yet." Everybody says "not yet." Everything is "not yet." Until it's "too late."
TASHA: Tommy...
TOMMY: I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm just... It's been twenty years. I told everybody I'd stay twenty more years, right here on this corner.
JESSE: Wait, there's more in the letter. Look, there's a second page.
TOMMY: What does it say? You read it, because I can't.
JESSE: "Please note that we will be conducting inspections of all units, including the commercial space, in September. Access will be required."
MARISOL: Inspections. That means they want to come inside, into every single apartment.
TASHA: Can they really do that? Can they just come inside our homes?
MARISOL: For repairs and inspections, yes, but only with notice, which is usually twenty-four hours in writing. They can't just walk in whenever they want.
TASHA: But what are they looking for in there?
MARISOL: Everything. They want to see the condition of each apartment, who lives where, how old the tenants are, and what they can renovate.
TOMMY: How old the tenants are. You mean people like Eddie.
MARISOL: Like Eddie, and like Dolores. They're looking for the long-term tenants with the low rents.
JESSE: And what about Three B? Kyle's apartment is empty now, isn't it?
MARISOL: Kyle's complaint is filed, so it's in the system. When they look, they'll find it. Good.
TASHA: Good? How is that good?
MARISOL: It's good because now they know we're not sleeping. We have an association, we have records, and we have a complaint on file.
TOMMY: And they have money. They have a lot of money.
MARISOL: They have money, but we have each other. And we have the law, or at least some of it.
JESSE: "Some of it." That's not very encouraging, Marisol.
MARISOL: It's honest, though. I don't want to lie to you, because this is going to be hard.
TASHA: How hard are we talking?
MARISOL: A year, maybe two. There'll be meetings, letters, and lawyers, and maybe court. Maybe noise and dust, too.
TOMMY: I'm fifty-eight years old. I don't have two years of fighting in me.
TASHA: You have twenty years of fighting in you. You've been a Mets fan for forty years, so you know how to suffer.
TOMMY: And I lost. Every single year, I lost.
JESSE: You didn't lose, though. You're still a fan, and that's not losing.
TOMMY: When did you get so wise, couch fella?
JESSE: I live in Gowanus now, with a lizard and two roommates. It changes a person.
TASHA: Okay. So what do we do? What do we do tonight, right now?
MARISOL: Tonight, nothing. We read the letters again, and we sleep. Tomorrow, the association meets, and we make a plan.
TOMMY: And Eddie? How's Eddie doing with all this?
MARISOL: Eddie's upstairs. His new air conditioner from the program arrived today, on the same day as the letter.
TASHA: The same day! That's... I honestly don't know if that's funny or sad.
TOMMY: It's both. That's this building for you. It's funny and sad, every single day.
JESSE: Can I say something? Even if it sounds a little corny?
TASHA: Go ahead, honey. We could use something corny right now.
JESSE: This spring, I didn't have a home, just a couch. This building gave me a home, and it still feels like home, even after I left.
TOMMY: Jesse...
JESSE: So if you need help, I'm here, on weekends and on weeknights. I can do spreadsheets, lemonade, anything you need.
TASHA: That's not corny, honey. That's beautiful.

[Scene: 11:00 p.m. The Hook is closed. Tommy sits alone at the bar. The door opens.]

GINA: Dad? Tasha called me and told me everything. Are you okay?
TOMMY: Gina. You didn't have to come all the way over here. It's late. Where's Luca?
GINA: He's sleeping, and my neighbor is watching him. I'm here now, so talk to me.
TOMMY: They sold it. They sold the building to the suit, that Grant Whitlock guy.
GINA: I know, because Tasha told me. And what about your lease? What about the bar?
TOMMY: It's good until May. After that, it's "current market conditions," which might mean fourteen thousand.
GINA: Fourteen... Dad. That's impossible.
TOMMY: That's exactly what I said.
GINA: Okay. Okay. We'll figure it out. You always say that, don't you? "We'll figure it out."
TOMMY: No, Marcus says that. I say, "What're ya gonna do?"
GINA: Then what are we gonna do, Dad?
TOMMY: I don't know, sweetheart. For the first time in twenty years, I really don't know.
GINA: Then we find out, together. I'm good at plans, and you know that. I'm the spreadsheet person.
TOMMY: You're good at everything, except resting.
GINA: I'm learning, though. Look, my phone is in my bag, and it's off. I turned it off for you.
TOMMY: Off. For me. Wow, this really is a strange day.
GINA: Wait. Your phone is ringing. Who calls at eleven at night?
TOMMY: It's an unknown number, so it's probably a scam. Eddie taught me what to do: hang up and call back.
GINA: Answer it anyway. Just listen, and don't say anything important.
TOMMY: Hello? This is Tommy Russo.
TERESA: Mr. Russo? I'm sorry to call so late. My name is Teresa. Teresa Cappello.
TOMMY: Teresa? Sal's daughter? Teresa, of course I remember you. How is your father?
TERESA: He's... the same. He doesn't know about any of this. Mr. Russo, I need to tell you something, and it isn't easy.
TOMMY: What is it? Go ahead, I'm listening.
TERESA: The note that went under your doors in May... I wrote it. It was me.
GINA: Dad? Dad, who is it? What's she saying?
TERESA: And there's something else, something about the sale. My brother didn't tell you the truth. Can we meet? Tomorrow?

## 표현 정리 (낭독하지 않음)

- **공문 표현 → 쉬운 말**
  - *Please be advised that…* → "~임을 알려 드립니다."
  - *an affiliate of* → "~의 계열사."
  - *Effective September first* → "9월 1일부터."
  - *the enclosed instructions* → "동봉한 안내문."
  - *will remain in full force and effect* → "그대로 유효하다."
  - *capital improvements* → "대규모 개보수(리모델링)."
  - *in due course* → "적절한 때에(= 나중에, 저쪽이 원할 때)."
  - *which will reflect current market conditions* → "현재 시세를 반영할 것" = 월세 대폭 인상.
  - *Access will be required.* → "출입이 필요할 것입니다(문을 열어 줘야 한다)."
- **a certified letter / sign for it** — 등기 우편 / 수령 서명을 하다.
- **the managing agent** — 건물 관리 회사.
- **충격 표현** — *Oh no. / It's real. It's really real. / That's impossible. / I can't stand it.*(못 참겠어, 애가 타)
- **That's something.** — "그나마 다행이네."
- **I don't have two years of fighting in me.** — "나한텐 2년을 싸울 힘이 없어."
- **corny** — 오글거리는, 진부한.
- **It changes a person.** — "사람이 달라지지."
- **We'll figure it out.** — "어떻게든 해결할 거야." 마커스의 말버릇.

## 낭독 포인트 (낭독하지 않음)

- **공문 낭독**: 마리솔과 제시가 읽는 부분은 감정 없이 일정한 높이로, 쉼표마다 쉬며 또박또박. 그 뒤 반응은 구어로 빠르게.
- **affiliate** [어**필**리엇], **capital improvements** [캐피를 임**프루**브먼츠], **in due course** [인 듀 코r스].
- **Fourteen thousand! | I pay sixty-five hundred!** — teen에 강세. 두 숫자를 대비해서.
- **"Not yet." … Until it's "too late."** — 토미의 감정이 처음으로 터지는 곳. 목소리가 커졌다가 *I'm sorry.*에서 바로 낮아진다.
- 마지막 전화 장면은 조용하게. 테레사의 목소리는 망설이며 작게, 마지막 *Can we meet? Tomorrow?*는 간절하게.
