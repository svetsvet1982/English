# THE CORNER HOUSE — 2부 26화

## Two Hundred and Fifty (250주년)

> **표기 전용 정보 (낭독하지 않음)**
> - 부/화: 2부 8화(통산 26화) · 시기: 2026년 7월 4일(토), 미국 건국 250주년 독립기념일 · 난이도: B1~B2 · 턴 수: 120
> - 장소: 레드훅 부두(오전, 범선 행렬) → 건물 뒷마당 바비큐(오후) → 부두(밤, 불꽃놀이)
> - 등장인물: DIEGO, EDDIE, MARISOL, TOMMY, PRIYA, SAM, ARTURO
> - 사건: 뉴욕항에 세계 각국의 대형 범선이 모인다. 에디는 50년 전 1976년 건국 200주년 때 같은 부두에서 범선을 봤다. 오후 바비큐에서 각자의 이민 내력을 나누고, 프리야와 샘이 결혼 소식을 전한다.
> - 학습 포인트: 행사·역사 어휘, 큰 숫자와 연도 읽기, 감탄, fewer vs less
> - 미국 현안: 건국 250주년(2026), 이민자의 나라라는 정체성(정치적 논쟁 없이 각자의 이야기로)
> - 연결: 프리야·샘 내년 봄 결혼, 부두 결혼식 아이디어 → 85화. 양복 남자가 화요일에 또 왔음. 디에고의 약속: 2076년에 이 부두로.

## 대본

[Scene: Saturday, July 4, 10:00 a.m. The pier in Red Hook. Crowds by the railing. Tall ships on the harbor.]

DIEGO: Eddie! Look over there! There's another one, and this one has three masts!
EDDIE: That's a tall ship, kid. It's a real sailing ship, like the ones they had in the old days.
MARISOL: How many are there in total? I've already counted twenty, and they keep coming.
EDDIE: The radio said more than forty, from all over the world. They're here for the two hundred and fiftieth birthday.
DIEGO: Wait, America is two hundred and fifty years old? Like, exactly?
EDDIE: Exactly, as of today. Seventeen seventy-six plus two hundred fifty makes July fourth, twenty twenty-six.
DIEGO: That's so old! It's even older than you!
EDDIE: It's a little older than me, but not by much. Some mornings I feel two hundred and fifty.
MARISOL: Diego, what happened in seventeen seventy-six? You learned about it in school this year.
DIEGO: The Declaration of Independence! A bunch of guys signed a paper in Philadelphia, and our teacher made us read part of it out loud.
EDDIE: Good. And do you remember what the paper said?
DIEGO: It said that America was free from England. And... "all men are created equal."
MARISOL: Very good. And we hope it means women too, because it should.
EDDIE: It took a while for that part, though. It took a long while.
DIEGO: Look at that one! The sails are white and red, like a candy cane!
EDDIE: I think that one's from Spain, because I can see the flag in the back.
MARISOL: It's so beautiful, with all the sails on the water and the Statue of Liberty right there behind them.
EDDIE: You know, I stood on this same pier fifty years ago, on this very same day. I watched the same kind of ships go by.
DIEGO: Fifty years ago? You mean in nineteen... seventy-six?
EDDIE: Nineteen seventy-six, the Bicentennial, which means two hundred years. They had tall ships then too, and they called it Operation Sail.
MARISOL: You saw all this before? The same thing, right here?
EDDIE: Almost the same. I was thirty-three, Peggy was standing next to me, and Michael was on my shoulders. He was four.
DIEGO: Your son was only four? But now he's old!
EDDIE: Now he's fifty-four, and he has a daughter in college. That's how fast it goes.
MARISOL: What was it like back then? In seventy-six, I mean.
EDDIE: It was crowded and hot, just like today. But the pier was different, because it was a working pier, with ships, cargo, and longshoremen everywhere.
DIEGO: You worked here? On this pier?
EDDIE: Right over there, where the IKEA is now. That was all docks, and I unloaded coffee from Brazil and bananas from Ecuador.
MARISOL: And now people go there to buy furniture. That's kind of funny, if you think about it.
EDDIE: Now people buy furniture and eat meatballs where I used to carry bananas. Times change.
DIEGO: Were there fireworks too? In seventy-six?
EDDIE: Huge fireworks, right over the harbor. They were the biggest I ever saw, until tonight, maybe.
MARISOL: So are you coming back tonight to watch them from here?
EDDIE: I wouldn't miss it. It's been fifty years, and it's the same pier. Different knees, but the same pier.
DIEGO: Eddie, how many people live in America right now?
EDDIE: Ask your mother, because she's smarter than me with numbers.
MARISOL: About three hundred and forty million. Back in seventeen seventy-six, there were only about two and a half million.
DIEGO: Two and a half million? That's less than Brooklyn!
MARISOL: Exactly. Brooklyn has about two and a half million people, so the whole country was the size of Brooklyn.
EDDIE: And they probably all spoke with Brooklyn accents, too.
DIEGO: Really?
EDDIE: No. I'm kidding.
MARISOL: Okay, let's go home. Tommy is starting the barbecue at two, and he needs help with the chairs.
DIEGO: Bye, ships! See you in two hundred fifty years!
EDDIE: Make it fifty, kid. Then you can come back here and tell your grandkids all about it.

[Scene: 3:00 p.m. The backyard behind the building. Tommy at an old grill. Flags on the fence.]

TOMMY: Burgers are almost ready, and the hot dogs will be up in five minutes! Who wants cheese?
PRIYA: Me! Cheese on everything, please. It's a holiday, so the usual rules don't count.
SAM: Tommy, these smell amazing. What do you put in them?
TOMMY: It's a secret. Okay, fine, it's salt, pepper, and a little love. And a grill from nineteen ninety-eight.
MARISOL: Priya, Sam, have you met my dad yet? Papi, this is Priya and Sam, from Four B upstairs.
ARTURO: Nice to meet you both. Mucho gusto.
PRIYA: Nice to meet you too! Happy Fourth!
ARTURO: Happy Fourth. This is my forty-second Fourth of July in America, if you can believe it.
SAM: Forty-second! Then you've seen a lot more of them than I have, because I'm only thirty-four.
ARTURO: And where are your families from, if I can ask?
PRIYA: My parents came from India, from Gujarat, in nineteen ninety. They opened a pharmacy in Edison, New Jersey.
ARTURO: A pharmacy! That's hard work, running a family business like that.
PRIYA: It's very hard, seven days a week. My dad still works there every day, and he's sixty-eight.
SAM: My family has been here longer. My great-great-grandparents came from Ireland and Germany in the eighteen eighties, and they settled in Ohio.
TOMMY: And mine came from Naples in nineteen ten. They moved to Columbia Street, which is right over there.
MARISOL: So everyone at this table came from somewhere else, if you go back far enough.
TOMMY: Everyone in this city came from somewhere. Except the people who were here first, of course, and we should remember them today.
ARTURO: That's true. Very true.
PRIYA: My dad says the Fourth of July is his favorite holiday, even more than Diwali. Don't tell my mom I said that.
SAM: Really? Why?
PRIYA: He says on Diwali, he celebrates where he came from. On the Fourth, he celebrates where he chose to be.
ARTURO: Your father is a wise man. That's exactly how I feel about this country.
MARISOL: Papi, really? You never told me that.
ARTURO: Really. I chose this place, even when it's hard and even when the news is scary. I chose it, and I would choose it again.
TOMMY: That's a good toast. Everybody, raise your drinks. To choosing.
PRIYA: To choosing!
SAM: To choosing. And to Tommy's burgers, which are the second best thing about today.
TOMMY: Thank you. Okay, Sam, take this plate, but be careful, because it's hot.
SAM: Thanks. Hey, Tommy, is there any news about the building? Priya told me about the note.
TOMMY: No news. Richie still doesn't call me back, and the guy in the suit came again on Tuesday.
PRIYA: Our lease ends in December, and we were thinking about... well, about staying longer. Maybe for a long time.
MARISOL: For a long time? Why? Is something happening that I don't know about?
PRIYA: We're getting married next spring!
MARISOL: Oh my God! Congratulations, you two!
TOMMY: Married! And we find out on the Fourth of July! Mazel tov! Congratulations!
SAM: Thanks. We wanted to tell everybody today, because it felt like a good day for news, and everybody we love is here.
ARTURO: ¡Felicidades! So where is the wedding going to be?
PRIYA: We don't know yet, but somewhere with water. Maybe even right here, on the pier.
TOMMY: On the pier! With the Statue of Liberty right behind you!
MARISOL: That would be perfect. I'm already crying a little, and it hasn't even happened yet.

[Scene: 9:28 p.m. The pier again. Darkness. Everyone looks at the sky.]

DIEGO: Is it starting? Is it starting?
EDDIE: Patience. It starts at nine thirty, so there are two more minutes.
TOMMY: The whole neighborhood is out here tonight. Just look at this crowd.
MARISOL: It's everybody from the block. Ahmed is here, and Keisha and Malik, and even people from the glass building.
EDDIE: Fifty years ago, there were even more people than this. But there were less phones.
DIEGO: Fewer phones, Eddie. You can count phones, so it's fewer.
EDDIE: Fewer phones. Thank you, professor.
PRIYA: Look! Over there, above the water! There's the first one!
DIEGO: Whoa! It's huge! And it's red!
TOMMY: Here they come. Oh, wow. Look at that.
MARISOL: Gold ones! They're coming down like rain, like gold rain!
DIEGO: Did you see that one? It was a heart, a heart in the sky!
EDDIE: In seventy-six, there were no hearts. There were just big circles that went boom, boom.
PRIYA: Listen to the crowd. Everybody's saying "oooh" at the same time.
TOMMY: "Oooh" is the same in every language. It's the one word everybody knows.
DIEGO: Now it's red, white, and blue, all together!
EDDIE: That's the one. That's the big one.
MARISOL: Eddie, are you okay? You've been very quiet for a while.
EDDIE: I'm okay. I'm just... I'm thinking about Peggy. She stood right here, next to me, in seventy-six.
MARISOL: She would have loved this, Eddie. I'm sure of it.
EDDIE: She would. She'd say, "Edward, it's too loud." And then she'd hold my hand anyway.
DIEGO: I'll hold your hand, Eddie, if you want. My hand is smaller, but it works.
EDDIE: Thank you, kid. I'd like that very much.
TOMMY: Look, the ships are lit up too. All the masts are covered in little lights.
PRIYA: It's like a painting, but a painting that moves.
DIEGO: Eddie, in fifty years, will there be fireworks again? For the three hundredth birthday?
EDDIE: In twenty seventy-six, for three hundred years? Yes, there will. They'll probably be even bigger.
DIEGO: Wait, I'll be sixty by then! I'll be old!
EDDIE: Sixty is not old, kid. Sixty is the middle, believe me.
MARISOL: And will you come back here, to this same pier?
DIEGO: Yes. And I'll bring my kids, and I'll tell them all about Eddie.
EDDIE: Tell them I said the coffee from Brazil came in right here, on this pier, on my back.
DIEGO: And I'll tell them that you saw the tall ships twice!
EDDIE: Twice. Not many people can say that, and someday you'll be one of them.
TOMMY: Okay, everybody. Happy two hundred fiftieth. Happy birthday, America.

## 표현 정리 (낭독하지 않음)

- **역사 어휘** — *the Declaration of Independence*(독립선언서), *sign*(서명하다), *free from England*, *all men are created equal*(모든 사람은 평등하게 태어났다), *the Bicentennial*(200주년), *the two hundred and fiftieth (birthday)*(250주년).
- **연도·큰 숫자 읽기** — *seventeen seventy-six / nineteen seventy-six / twenty twenty-six / twenty seventy-six* / *three hundred and forty million / two and a half million*.
- **서수 + 행사** — *my forty-second Fourth of July / the two hundred and fiftieth birthday*.
- **fewer vs less** — 셀 수 있는 명사는 fewer(fewer phones), 셀 수 없는 명사는 less(less noise). 구어에서는 less를 섞어 쓰는 사람도 많아서 디에고가 '교수님'처럼 고쳐 준다.
- **It took a while.** — "시간이 좀 걸렸지."
- **I wouldn't miss it.** — "절대 놓치지 않을 거야."
- **Make it fifty.** — "50으로 하자(50년 뒤로 정정)."
- **Mucho gusto. / ¡Felicidades! / Mazel tov!** — 스페인어 "반가워요", "축하해요", 이디시어 "축하해"(뉴욕 사람들이 출신과 상관없이 쓴다).
- **Raise your drinks. To ~!** — 건배 표현.
- **Patience.** — "참아, 기다려."
- **lit up** — 불이 밝혀진.
- **감탄** — *Whoa! / Look at that. / Oh, wow. / That's the big one.*

## 낭독 포인트 (낭독하지 않음)

- **연도는 두 자리씩**: **seventeen | seventy-six**, **twenty | twenty-six**. 200주년 *Bicentennial* [바이센**테**니얼].
- **two hundred and fiftieth** [투 헌드러드 앤 **피**프티어θ] — 마지막 th를 혀끝으로.
- **Declaration of Independence** [데클러**레**이션 어브 인디**펜**던스] — 두 군데 강세.
- 불꽃놀이 장면은 짧은 감탄을 높게, 에디의 회상은 낮고 느리게. 대비가 장면을 만든다.
- **"Oooh"**는 다 같이 길게 내린다.
- 마지막 대사 *Happy birthday, America.*는 생일 축하처럼 따뜻하게.
