# THE CORNER HOUSE — 5부 78화

## The Renovation (공사)

> **표기 전용 정보 (낭독하지 않음)**
> - 부/화: 5부 6화(통산 78화) · 시기: 2027년 5월 17일(월) ~ 19일(수) · 난이도: B2~C1 · 턴 수: 120
> - 장소: 아침의 2B → 인도의 비계(사이드워크 셰드) 아래 → 공사 중인 4C → 수요일 저녁의 인도
> - 등장인물: MRS. ESPOSITO, EDDIE, AHMED, TOMMY, MARCUS, KOWALSKI, MARISOL, MR. HAYES, TASHA, JESSE, SAM, DIEGO
> - 사건: 조합이 소유한 뒤 첫 공사가 시작된다. 4C를 고치고, 물이 새는 외벽 벽돌 줄눈을 다시 메운다. 월요일 아침 7시부터 드릴 소리가 울리고, 건물 앞 인도에는 비계 지붕이 선다. 길 건너 아메드는 비계 때문에 가게 간판이 안 보인다고 항의한다. 토미의 'The Hook' 간판도 가려졌다. 아메드는 "코트 스트리트 비계는 9년째 서 있다"며 믿지 못한다. 코왈스키는 3주 안에 철거하겠다고 서면으로 약속한다. 화요일, 4C의 우선 신청자 헤이스 씨가 방을 보러 온다. 다니던 건물이 팔린 뒤 누나 집 소파에서 지내 왔다. 수요일 저녁, 아이들이 비계 판자에 벽화를 그리고 두 가게의 이름을 크게 써 넣는다.
> - 학습 포인트: 불평과 완곡 표현(It's a bit much. / I don't mean to complain, but… / It's not ideal. / Would you mind…? / With all due respect, it's been…), 사과와 약속(I hear you. / We'll make it right. / You have my word. / In writing.), 건축 어휘(sidewalk shed, scaffolding, repointing, mortar, permit, debris)
> - 미국 현안: 뉴욕시의 오래 방치된 인도 비계 문제, 공사 소음 규정(평일 오전 7시~오후 6시), 작은 상점에 미치는 공사 영향, 이웃 간의 신뢰
> - 연결: 76화 4C 간단 공사 1.2만 승인, '주거 불안정 우선' 기준. 58화 헤이스 씨(B61 기사, SNAP 이의 신청). 44화 프라임 빌드의 공사 쓰레기. 25화 아메드 가게(고양이 술탄). 77화 태양광 5/26 설치 예정.

## 대본

[Scene: Monday, May 17, 7:02 a.m. Apartment 2B. A drill screams somewhere above. Mrs. Esposito sits at her kitchen table with her hands over her ears. Eddie comes in with two cups of coffee.]

MRS. ESPOSITO: Seven a.m., exactly. Not six fifty-nine, and not one minute early, because they're very legal.
EDDIE: The city allows construction from seven a.m. on weekdays. Brian told us that three times, in case we forgot.
MRS. ESPOSITO: I know it's legal, Eddie. It's still a bit much.
EDDIE: It's a bit much. I'll give you that, because my coffee is shaking in the cup.
MRS. ESPOSITO: I don't mean to complain, but we voted for this. I voted for this, and I wrote "4C, simple version" in my own minutes.
EDDIE: You can complain, Dolores. We own the place now, so we're allowed to complain about ourselves.
MRS. ESPOSITO: That's the strange part. When Harborline made noise, I was angry. Now we make the noise, and I feel guilty for being angry.
EDDIE: That's ownership for you. Guilt with a mortgage.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Ha! Well, Biscuit is under the bed, and she's been there since seven. She won't come out until six p.m.
EDDIE: Smart cat. She knows the city rules too: seven to six, and not a minute more.
MRS. ESPOSITO: And Mr. Hayes is coming tomorrow to see 4C. Gloria called me last night to set it up.
EDDIE: The bus driver? The one from the pantry?
MRS. ESPOSITO: That's him. He's the first applicant with the "unstable housing" priority, and he's been on his sister's couch since February.
EDDIE: Since February? Then a little noise is worth it.
MRS. ESPOSITO: That's what I keep telling myself, every time the drill starts. "Mr. Hayes. Mr. Hayes."
EDDIE: It's like a prayer, with a drill.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Brian promised three weeks of this, for 4C and the bricks together.
EDDIE: Three weeks? In Brooklyn construction, three weeks means five, and five means eight.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Don't say that. I'll write it down, and then it'll be true.

[Scene: 10:00 a.m. The sidewalk in front of the building. A green wooden sidewalk shed now covers the whole front. Its roof hides the top half of The Hook's sign. Ahmed crosses Van Brunt Street with his arms crossed.]

AHMED: Tommy! Tommy! Come out here and look at this. Look at this thing!
TOMMY: Good morning, Ahmed. I know, I know, it's ugly.
AHMED: It's not ugly, it's a wall. From my store, I can't see your door, and your customers can't see my store. It's a green wall across the whole corner.
TOMMY: It's a sidewalk shed, for the bricks. They're fixing the outside of the building, so that bricks don't fall on people's heads.
AHMED: I understand bricks, but I don't understand forever. You know the shed on Court Street, by the bank? It's been there nine years. Nine years, Tommy. Kids were born under that shed, and they're in third grade now.
MARCUS: He's right, Tommy. There are hundreds of these all over the city. They go up for a month, they stay for a decade, and nobody takes them down.
TOMMY: This one is different, because this one belongs to us. We're the owners now.
AHMED: That's what every owner says. "This one is different."
MARCUS: And Tommy, with respect, it's not ideal. Not for Ahmed, and not for me. My bikes are under the shed now, so nobody can see them from the street. I sold two bikes on Saturday, and today, zero.
TOMMY: Okay, okay. I hear you. Both of you. Brian! Brian, come over here, because we need you.
KOWALSKI: Good morning, everyone. I'm guessing this is about the shed.
AHMED: Good guess. So how long, Mr. Property Manager? Honestly?
KOWALSKI: Three weeks. The masons start the bricks on Wednesday, and it's ten working days. Then the shed comes down.
AHMED: With all due respect, I heard "three weeks" on Court Street too. That was in two thousand eighteen, Brian.
KOWALSKI: I know, because I used to be on the other side of that sentence. At Bridgewater, I said "three weeks" all the time, and I didn't mean it. This time, I mean it.
AHMED: And why should I believe you?
KOWALSKI: Because I'll put it in writing, with a date: June eighth. If the shed is still up on June eighth, the co-op pays you two hundred dollars a week for lost business.
TOMMY: We do? Since when do we do that?
KOWALSKI: Since right now, if the board agrees. Mrs. Esposito?
MRS. ESPOSITO: I'm the secretary, not the board. But I'll call Marisol in two minutes, and Sam too. He'll say yes, because he loves a penalty clause.
AHMED: Two hundred a week, in writing, and signed?
KOWALSKI: It'll be signed. You have my word. And my signature is better than my word. I learned that this year.
MARCUS: And what about my bikes?
KOWALSKI: We'll hang a sign on the shed with big letters: "Bell Bikes — Open." There'll be one for The Hook and one for Ahmed's too, all at eye level.
AHMED: Hm, a sign is something. It's not everything, but it's something.
TOMMY: Ahmed, I'll make it right. Free coffee for you, for three whole weeks.
AHMED: Tommy, your coffee is not compensation. Your coffee is another complaint.

[Scene: Tuesday, May 18, 12:15 p.m. Apartment 4C. Dust in the air. Fresh plaster on one wall. A new window lets in bright light. Kowalski, Marisol, and Eddie show the room to Mr. Hayes, the retired bus driver.]

KOWALSKI: Watch your step, Mr. Hayes. The floor is new, and the boards are still drying.
MR. HAYES: Okay, I'll go slowly, because of my back. Oh. Oh, look at that light.
MARISOL: That window was painted shut for six years, until Brian opened it yesterday. It faces south, so you can see the harbor from the corner.
MR. HAYES: I can see the harbor, and the B61 stop. Look, right there on Van Brunt. I drove that bus for twenty-six years.
EDDIE: Then you'll always know when it's late, which is always.
MR. HAYES: It was never late when I drove it. Well, almost never.
MARISOL: Mr. Hayes, you're the first applicant with the "unstable housing" priority. Your file is complete, because Dolores and Gloria helped with the paperwork. If you want it, the city has to approve, which takes about a week.
MR. HAYES: If I want it? Ms. Reyes, I've been sleeping on my sister's couch in Canarsie since February. My building in Bed-Stuy was sold, and the new owner didn't renew anybody.
EDDIE: That's just like Harborline. It's the same story, on a different street.
MR. HAYES: The same story. My sister is kind, but she's seventy, and her couch is old. My back is even older.
KOWALSKI: The rent is based on your income, at about thirty percent. With your pension, that's about six hundred and fifty a month.
MR. HAYES: Six fifty? I can do six fifty. I can't do anything else in this city, but I can do six fifty.
MARISOL: There's something you should know about this room before you decide. A long time ago, it was somebody's room.
MR. HAYES: Whose room?
EDDIE: It was Lucia Cappello's, the landlord's wife. She sewed in here for forty years, including Halloween costumes for every kid on the block. When she died in twenty twenty-one, her husband couldn't rent it, so he kept it empty for her.
MR. HAYES: And now you want to give it to an old bus driver?
MARISOL: He left a letter, and he wrote, "Give 4C to someone who needs it, someone with no place to go." That's you, Mr. Hayes, if you'll have it.
MR. HAYES: ...Can I sit down? Is there a chair?
KOWALSKI: There's a paint bucket, and it's clean. Here you go.
MR. HAYES: Thank you. My mother sewed too, back in Georgia, on a machine with a pedal. That was before she came north in nineteen sixty-one.
EDDIE: Then maybe the room will feel familiar to you.
MR. HAYES: I'd like to keep something of hers, of Lucia's, if that's okay. Just something small. So the room remembers.
MARISOL: Her sewing box is in the basement, and Dolores has it now. I'm sure she'd want it to come back here, to the windowsill.
MR. HAYES: The windowsill, with the harbor. Yes, that's right. That's where it belongs.
EDDIE: Mr. Hayes, there's one more thing. You should know what you're getting into with the neighbors.
MR. HAYES: Tell me. I've had bad neighbors before, so I can handle anything.
EDDIE: Down the hall, you've got Marisol and Diego, and Diego is loud. It's in the official ledger. Across the hall, there's Priya and Sam, and probably a dog soon.
MARISOL: And on the second floor, there's Dolores. If she doesn't hear from you by nine, she'll come and check on you.
MR. HAYES: She'll check on me?
EDDIE: She knocks on my floor every morning, from below: two knocks. If I don't answer, she comes up with a key.
MR. HAYES: In my old building, I didn't know anybody's name. That was eleven years, and not one name.
KOWALSKI: Here, you'll know everybody's name in eleven days, whether you want to or not.
MR. HAYES: I want to, believe me. I really want to.

[Scene: Tuesday, 4:00 p.m. The Hook. A fine layer of brick dust on the bar. Tommy wipes it. Tasha studies a chemistry book. Jesse and Sam sit with laptops. Marcus comes in from next door.]

TASHA: Tommy, I can't study. Every time I read a sentence, the drill reads it with me.
TOMMY: The drilling is only in 4C, and there are two more days of it. Then the masons start on the bricks, which is quieter, at least a little.
SAM: Two more days? I've had four video calls today, and my boss thinks I work in a factory.
JESSE: My calls are worse. I'm training pantry volunteers in Queens, and they hear "BRRRRT" every thirty seconds. One lady asked me if I was okay.
MARCUS: I sold one bike today. Just one, to a guy who was lost.
TOMMY: Lunch was down too, about forty percent. People don't like walking under a shed, because it feels like a tunnel.
TASHA: It is a tunnel. It's a green tunnel, with dust.
SAM: Okay, I'll say the treasurer thing. Every week the shed stays up, we lose money: the bar, Marcus, and maybe Ahmed's two hundred dollars.
JESSE: So we make it worth looking at. We make people want to walk under it.
MARCUS: And how do we do that?
JESSE: With art. The shed is plywood and paper, so it's a giant canvas. Diego and Malik could paint it with kids from the school, and I'll post it online: "Red Hook kids paint the ugliest shed in Brooklyn."
TASHA: That's actually a good idea, for a man who just lost a call to a drill.
TOMMY: Paint? On our shed? Is that even allowed?
SAM: We rent the shed from the scaffolding company, but the paper on it is ours. Brian already checked.
JESSE: So it's technically allowed, which is my favorite kind of allowed.
MARCUS: And put the store names on it, nice and big. Put Ahmed's on too, so he stops yelling at Tommy.
TOMMY: Ahmed doesn't yell. Ahmed explains loudly.
TASHA: I'll call Ms. Park, because she's always looking for "community projects." This is a community, and it's a project.

[Scene: Wednesday, May 19, 6:30 p.m. The sidewalk. The masons have gone home. The green shed is covered with brown paper. Diego, Malik, and three other kids paint with big brushes. Ahmed watches from his doorway. Tommy and Kowalski stand nearby. Mrs. Esposito supervises with her clipboard.]

DIEGO: Ahmed! Ahmed! Come and look! We're painting your store on the shed!
AHMED: My store? On the shed?
DIEGO: Look, here's your door, and here's your cat, Sultan, sleeping on the bread. And there are big letters: "AHMED'S — OPEN — ACROSS THE STREET."
AHMED: Sultan is on the bread? Diego, that's a violation of the health code.
DIEGO: It's art, and art doesn't have health codes.
TOMMY: And look, they painted the bar too. "THE HOOK — OPEN — BAD COFFEE, GOOD PEOPLE."
AHMED: Ha! That's honest. That's the most honest sign on Van Brunt Street.
KOWALSKI: And here's the paper, Ahmed, signed by the board. Marisol and Sam signed it this afternoon: "Shed down by June eighth, or two hundred dollars per week."
AHMED: Signed, okay. Thank you, Brian. I'll put it on my wall, next to my first dollar.
MRS. ESPOSITO: That's number six on the owners' list. "Shed agreement. Ahmed. Signed. May nineteenth."
AHMED: Mrs. Esposito, you have a new list?
MRS. ESPOSITO: The old list was about Harborline's problems, and it had forty-nine items. This list is about our problems, and there are six so far.
AHMED: Six problems in three weeks? That's not bad, for owners.
MRS. ESPOSITO: It's not bad, but I'm keeping count. Somebody has to keep the owners honest, even when the owners are us.
TOMMY: Ahmed, can I ask you something? Honestly?
AHMED: Honestly? Go ahead, ask.
TOMMY: If the shed comes down on time... will you trust us? I mean the co-op, the next time we make noise.
AHMED: Tommy, I've been across the street from you for nineteen years. I trust you. I don't trust sheds. There's a difference.
TOMMY: Fair enough. I can live with that.
DIEGO: Ahmed! Can I paint Biscuit too, next to Sultan?
AHMED: Biscuit and Sultan together, on one wall? They hate each other!
MRS. ESPOSITO: They don't hate each other; they have boundaries. It's like the dog and the cat, and it's in the rules.
DIEGO: Then I'll paint a line between them, a small one. Like Switzerland.

## 표현 정리 (낭독하지 않음)

- **불평과 완곡 표현**
  - *It's a bit much.* — "좀 심하네." 직접적인 불만을 부드럽게.
  - *I don't mean to complain, but…* — "불평하려는 건 아니지만…"
  - *It's not ideal.* — "이상적이진 않네." 매우 완곡한 불만.
  - *With all due respect, I heard "three weeks" on Court Street too.* — 정중하지만 날카로운 반박.
- **사과와 약속**
  - *I hear you. Both of you.* — "둘 다 무슨 말인지 알아."
  - *I'll make it right.* — "제대로 바로잡을게."
  - *You have my word.* — "약속해."
  - *My signature is better than my word.* — 코왈스키가 1년 동안 배운 것. 말보다 서면.
- **건축 어휘**
  - *sidewalk shed* 인도 보호 비계 / *scaffolding* 비계 / *masons* 석공·벽돌공 / *bricks* 벽돌
  - *penalty clause* 위약 조항 / *working days* 작업일 / *plaster* 회반죽 / *painted shut* 페인트로 붙어 안 열리는
- **Guilt with a mortgage.** — "대출 딸린 죄책감." 주인이 된다는 것에 대한 에디의 농담.
- **I trust you. I don't trust sheds.** — "너는 믿어. 비계는 안 믿어." 사람과 제도를 구분하는 말.
- **So the room remembers.** — "방이 기억하도록." 헤이스 씨의 말.

## 낭독 포인트 (낭독하지 않음)

- 완곡 표현은 억양을 낮추고 끝을 살짝 흐린다: *It's a bit much...↘* / *It's not ideal...↘*
- 아메드의 항의는 크고 빠르게, 그러나 *Nine years, Tommy.*에서 멈추고 무게를 둔다.
- 공사 소음 속 대사라는 설정: 첫 장면은 조금 더 크게, 짧게 끊어서.
- 4C 장면은 조용하게. 헤이스 씨의 *Oh, look at that light.*는 숨을 들이켜듯.
- *Can I sit down? Is there a chair?* — 감정이 북받쳐 말이 짧아진다.
- 마지막 디에고의 *Like Switzerland.* — 76화의 중립 지대 농담을 아이가 이어받는다. 밝게.
