# THE CORNER HOUSE — 3부 53화

## A Goodbye (작별)

> **표기 전용 정보 (낭독하지 않음)**
> - 부/화: 3부 17화(통산 53화) · 시기: 2026년 11월 18일(수) · 난이도: B1~B2 · 턴 수: 120
> - 장소: 비지테이션 성당 앞 계단 → 더 훅(장례 후 모임) → 2층과 3층 사이 계단
> - 등장인물: EDDIE, MRS. ESPOSITO, MAUREEN, SONNY, TOMMY, TASHA
> - 사건: 에디의 부두 시절 선배 지미 도일(86)이 세상을 떠났다. 커피 스트리트에서 혼자 살던 지미는 이틀 뒤에야 우체부에게 발견됐다. 장례 미사 뒤 딸 모린이 에디에게 추도사를 부탁한다. 오후에 더 훅에서 아일랜드식 추모 모임이 열린다. 에디는 1968년 첫 출근 날 지미가 갈고리 쓰는 법을 가르쳐 준 이야기, 컨테이너선이 들어와 부두 일이 뉴저지로 떠나던 시절 이야기를 한다. 밤에는 '혼자 죽는 것'이 두려운 에디에게 부인이 약속을 제안한다. 매일 아침 8시 바닥을 두 번 두드리기.
> - 학습 포인트: 애도 표현(I'm so sorry for your loss / We'll miss you / My condolences / Thank you for coming), 추도사(He was the kind of man who… / I'll never forget… / If Jimmy were here, he'd say…), 조용한 어조와 회상
> - 미국 현안: 혼자 사는 노인의 고독사, 장례 비용, 컨테이너화 이후 사라진 브루클린 부두 일자리, 고령화하는 동네
> - 연결: 대니 월시도 커피 스트리트 출신(15화). 에디의 부두 = 지금의 이케아 자리(26화). 에디는 11화 이후 스마트폰 사용. 43화 레빈 박사·클립보드. 부인은 2B, 에디는 바로 위 3A.

## 대본

[Scene: Wednesday, 11:15 a.m. The steps outside Visitation Church on Verona Street. Cold wind. People in dark coats come out after the funeral Mass.]

MRS. ESPOSITO: Eddie, here. Take my arm, please, because the steps are slippery this morning.
EDDIE: I'm fine, Dolores. I've been walking down these steps for fifty years.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Then I want you to walk down them for fifty more. Slowly.
MAUREEN: Mr. O'Neill? Eddie? I'm Maureen, Jimmy's daughter. I don't know if you remember me.
EDDIE: Maureen. Of course I know you. I held you at your christening, right inside that door, and you screamed the whole time.
MAUREEN: Dad told me that story a hundred times. He said I had "longshoreman lungs."
EDDIE: I'm so sorry for your loss, sweetheart. He was a good man. He was the best of us.
MAUREEN: Thank you, and thank you for coming. It means a lot, because he talked about you all the time.
MRS. ESPOSITO: My condolences, Maureen. I'm Dolores, and I live in Eddie's building, one floor down.
MAUREEN: Thank you, Dolores. Thank you both for being here today.
SONNY: Eddie O'Neill, still standing. To be honest, I thought you'd be next.
EDDIE: Sonny Russo! Look at you. You're eighty-eight years old, and you're still insulting people.
SONNY: Well, somebody has to do it, now that Jimmy's not here anymore.
MAUREEN: Sonny worked with Dad too, didn't he? It was Pier Nine, right?
SONNY: Pier Nine, starting in nineteen fifty-eight. I was there before both of them, so I'm the oldest fossil on the waterfront.
EDDIE: Sonny taught Jimmy, and then Jimmy taught me. It's like a family tree, just with more bad words.
MAUREEN: Eddie, can I ask you something? It's kind of a big favor.
EDDIE: Anything, sweetheart. Just ask.
MAUREEN: We're having a little gathering at The Hook this afternoon. Would you say a few words about Dad?
EDDIE: Me? Give a speech? Maureen, I'm not good with speeches. I never have been.
MAUREEN: You don't need to be good. You just need to be true. You knew him longer than anyone, except Sonny.
SONNY: Don't look at me. When I give speeches, people cry, and it's the wrong kind of crying.
EDDIE: Okay. I'll try my best. I'll do it for Jimmy.
MAUREEN: Thank you. I keep thinking that I should have called him more. I lived out in Massapequa, and I only came once a month.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Oh, don't do that to yourself, dear. It won't help anybody.
MAUREEN: But he was alone for two days, Dolores. Two days, before the mail carrier noticed that the mail was piling up.
EDDIE: I know. I heard about it, and I haven't stopped thinking about it.
MAUREEN: He didn't want to move. I asked him ten times. I said, "Come live with us, Dad." And he always said, "My street is here."
EDDIE: That was Jimmy, all right. He was as stubborn as a rusty bolt.
SONNY: We're all like that, the old dock rats. We don't leave the neighborhood. We get carried out.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Sonny! Not today, of all days.
SONNY: Sorry. I'm sorry, Maureen. I always talk too much when I'm sad.
MAUREEN: It's okay, really. Dad would laugh at that. He'd say, "Sonny, shut your mouth and buy me a beer."
SONNY: He said that every day for sixty years, and I think I bought him maybe ten beers.

[Scene: 2:00 p.m. The Hook. A photo of Jimmy in his work jacket stands on the bar. About forty people. Tommy taps a glass.]

TOMMY: Okay, everybody, can I have your attention for a minute? Thank you all for coming. Jimmy Doyle drank here for twenty years.
TASHA: Every Tuesday, two beers, never three. And he always left a dollar under the coaster for me.
TOMMY: Maureen asked Eddie to say a few words about her father. Eddie, whenever you're ready.
EDDIE: Thank you, Tommy. Okay. I wrote some notes on a napkin, which is actually Tasha's napkin.
TASHA: Take your time, Eddie. Nobody's in a hurry today.
EDDIE: Jimmy Doyle was the kind of man who noticed things. If your boots were wrong, he noticed. If your heart was wrong, he noticed that too.
EDDIE: I met him in nineteen sixty-eight, right after I came home from Vietnam. I had no job, I couldn't sleep, and I was angry at everybody.
EDDIE: My father knew Jimmy's father, so one morning I went down to the pier. Jimmy looked at me and said, "You're the soldier. Can you lift?" I said yes. He said, "Then lift."
SONNY: That's Jimmy, all right. Two words, and that was all he ever needed. Then lift.
EDDIE: That first day, he showed me how to use the hook, the longshoreman's hook, which we used for the coffee sacks from Brazil.
EDDIE: He said, "Don't fight the sack. Let it sit on your back. Let your legs do the work." I'll never forget that.
EDDIE: Later on, I understood that he wasn't only talking about coffee. He was talking about everything, like grief, and bills, and life. Let your legs do the work.
MAUREEN: He said that to me too, when I had my first baby and I was so scared.
EDDIE: Then, in the seventies, the containers came. They were big steel boxes, and one crane did the work of fifty men. The ships went to Port Newark in New Jersey, and the jobs went with them.
SONNY: Thousands of men. Gone. And it all happened in about ten years.
EDDIE: A lot of guys gave up. Some drank, and some left. But Jimmy didn't give up, because he fought for every hour and for every man. He went to every union meeting with a thermos and a loud voice.
TASHA: He still had that loud voice, believe me. Every Tuesday, you could hear him from the kitchen.
EDDIE: When my Peggy died, I didn't leave my apartment for a week. Jimmy came every day, but he didn't say much. He just sat on my couch, watched hockey, and made terrible tea.
TOMMY: Was it worse than my coffee?
EDDIE: It was close, Tommy. Very close.
EDDIE: On the seventh day, he stood up and said, "Eddie. Let your legs do the work." Then he took me to the bar. He took me to this bar.
EDDIE: If Jimmy were here today, he'd say, "Stop crying and drink something. And somebody call my daughter, because she worries."
MAUREEN: That's exactly what he'd say, word for word.
EDDIE: Jimmy, you were the kind of friend a man gets only once, if he's lucky. I was lucky. Thank you. Rest now, and let somebody else do the lifting.
TOMMY: Everybody, raise your glasses. To Jimmy Doyle.
TASHA: To Jimmy. We'll miss you on Tuesdays.
SONNY: To Jimmy. You still owe me fifty beers, you old goat.
MAUREEN: Eddie, that was beautiful. Thank you so much. I didn't even know half of those stories.
EDDIE: He never talked about himself, and that was his only fault. Well, that and the tea.
MAUREEN: Can I tell you something? The funeral home costs twelve thousand dollars. Twelve thousand, and that's for three days.
TASHA: Twelve thousand dollars? Just for a funeral?
MAUREEN: And that's the simple one. Dad had a little insurance from the union, and it covered half. My husband and I paid the rest.
SONNY: When I go, just put me in a coffee sack and throw me in the harbor. It's free.
TOMMY: You know that's illegal, Sonny.
SONNY: So arrest me. I'll be dead, so good luck with that.
TOMMY: Maureen, the bar is paying for today. The food, the drinks, all of it is on us.
MAUREEN: Tommy, no. You really don't have to—
TOMMY: Jimmy left a dollar under that coaster every Tuesday for twenty years. That's about a thousand dollars, so I'm just paying him back.
MAUREEN: Thank you, Tommy. Dad loved this place. He always said it was the last real bar in Brooklyn.
TASHA: He said that to everybody who would listen. Every single Tuesday.
MAUREEN: Eddie, there's one more thing. Dad left something for you in his will.
EDDIE: For me? Jimmy had a will? I never thought he'd write anything down.
MAUREEN: It's one page, and it's handwritten. He left you his hook, the old one from Pier Nine.
EDDIE: His hook? He kept it, after all these years?
MAUREEN: He wrote, "For Eddie. He'll know what to do with it."
EDDIE: I'll hang it over my door, right next to Peggy's picture, so I see it every morning.
SONNY: And what about me? Did he leave me anything at all?
MAUREEN: He left you his bar tab, Sonny.
TOMMY: Now everybody, let's sing one song for him. It's Jimmy's song, and Sonny, I know you know it.
SONNY: "The Parting Glass." Sure, I know it. My voice is terrible, but I know every word.
EDDIE: Sing it, Sonny. Terrible is fine, because Jimmy sang it terrible too.
SONNY: Okay, but everybody has to help me. Sing it slowly, like it's the last glass of the night.

[Scene: 9:30 p.m. The staircase between the second and third floors. Eddie sits on a step. Mrs. Esposito comes out of 2B in her robe.]

MRS. ESPOSITO: Eddie? What are you doing out here on the stairs? It's cold in this hallway.
EDDIE: I'm resting. The second floor is my halfway house. Dr. Levine says I should rest on the way up.
MRS. ESPOSITO: You've been sitting here for twenty minutes, Eddie. I could hear you through the door.
EDDIE: Okay, you caught me. I'm not only resting. I'm thinking.
MRS. ESPOSITO: About Jimmy?
EDDIE: About the two days. Jimmy was on his kitchen floor for two days, and nobody knew.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Oh, Eddie.
EDDIE: I'm not afraid of dying, Dolores. I'm eighty-three, and I made my peace with that a long time ago. But I'm afraid of that. The two days alone, with the mail piling up.
MRS. ESPOSITO: I understand, because I think about it too. I live alone too, with a cat who doesn't call anyone.
EDDIE: Biscuit would eat me before she called anyone.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Probably. She's a very practical cat.
EDDIE: Jimmy's hook is in my coat pocket. Maureen gave it to me before she left.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Can I see it? Oh, my. It's heavy, and it's still sharp after all these years.
EDDIE: Michael keeps saying, "Move to Florida, Dad." Maybe he's right, and maybe I'm just being stubborn, like Jimmy.
MRS. ESPOSITO: You're being Eddie, and that's different. But listen to me, because I have an idea.
EDDIE: Of course you do. You always have an idea, and usually a clipboard too.
MRS. ESPOSITO: No clipboard this time, I promise. This one is simple. Every morning at eight o'clock, you knock on your floor twice with your cane.
EDDIE: But my floor is your ceiling.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Exactly. If I hear two knocks, I know you're alive. Then I knock back twice with my broom, so you know I'm alive too.
EDDIE: And what happens if there's no knock?
MRS. ESPOSITO: Then I come upstairs. I've had your spare key since two thousand twelve, since Sandy.
EDDIE: I completely forgot that you had that.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Well, I didn't forget. I never forget a key.
EDDIE: Eight o'clock, two knocks. But what if I oversleep one day?
MRS. ESPOSITO: You haven't overslept since nineteen seventy-four. You wake up at six every day, because I hear your radio.
EDDIE: You hear my radio? Every single morning?
MRS. ESPOSITO: Every morning. First the weather, and then the Irish music on Sundays. I like it, actually.
EDDIE: You never told me that, Dolores.
MRS. ESPOSITO: You never asked. Now, are you going to sit here all night? Or are you going to let your legs do the work?
EDDIE: So you were listening at the bar.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Everybody was listening, Eddie. It was a good speech, and Jimmy would be proud of you.
EDDIE: Okay. Help me up, but slowly. Tomorrow, eight o'clock. Two knocks.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Two knocks. And Eddie? Two knocks back, every day, for as long as we're both here.
EDDIE: As long as we're both here. That's a deal, Dolores.

## 표현 정리 (낭독하지 않음)

- **애도 표현**
  - *I'm so sorry for your loss, sweetheart.* — 가장 기본적인 조의 표현. 유족에게.
  - *My condolences, Maureen.* — "삼가 조의를 표합니다." 조금 더 격식 있다.
  - *He was a good man. / We'll miss you on Tuesdays.* — 고인을 기릴 때.
  - *It means a lot, because he talked about you all the time.* — 유족의 답례.
  - *Oh, don't do that to yourself, dear.* — "자책하지 마."
- **추도사 표현**
  - *Jimmy Doyle was the kind of man who noticed things.* — "그는 ~하는 사람이었다." 고인의 성품을 한 문장으로.
  - *I'll never forget that.* — "그건 절대 잊지 못할 겁니다."
  - *If Jimmy were here today, he'd say…* — 가정법 과거로 고인의 목소리를 불러온다.
  - *Rest now, and let somebody else do the lifting.* — "이제 편히 쉬어요."
  - *To Jimmy Doyle.* — 건배사. "지미 도일을 위하여."
- **longshoreman** — 항만 노동자. *Pier Nine* 9번 부두. *dock rats* 부둣가 사람들(자조적 별명).
- **Stubborn as a rusty bolt.** — "녹슨 볼트처럼 고집 세다."
- **Let your legs do the work.** — "다리가 일하게 해." 이 화의 핵심 문장. 힘든 것과 싸우지 말고 버티는 법.
- **made my peace with** — ~을 받아들였다.
- **As long as we're both here.** — "우리 둘 다 여기 있는 한."
- **you old goat** — "이 늙은 염소야." 오랜 친구 사이의 애정 섞인 욕. 친한 사이에서만, 웃으면서.

## 낭독 포인트 (낭독하지 않음)

- 이 화는 전체적으로 작은 음량, 느린 템포로 읽는다.
- **I'm so sorry for your loss.** — *so*에 강세, 끝은 내려서 조용히. 빠르게 말하면 형식적으로 들린다.
- 에디의 추도사는 한 문장마다 쉰다. *He said, "Then lift."* 뒤에서 2초 쉼.
- **Let your legs do the work.** — 추도사에서 세 번 나온다. 매번 조금씩 더 천천히.
- 소니는 거칠고 빠르게, 그러나 *Thousands of men. Gone.*에서는 목소리를 떨어뜨린다.
- 마지막 장면은 속삭이듯. *Two knocks.*를 말할 때 손가락으로 두 번 두드리는 리듬을 상상한다.

## 한글 해석

에스포지토 부인: 에디, 여기요. 내 팔 잡아요. 오늘 아침은 계단이 미끄러우니까.
에디: 난 괜찮아요, 돌로레스. 이 계단을 50년 동안 오르내렸어요.
에스포지토 부인: 그럼 앞으로 50년 더 내려가세요. 천천히요.
모린: 오닐 씨? 에디? 저 모린이에요, 지미 딸이요. 기억하실지 모르겠네요.
에디: 모린. 당연히 알지. 세례식 때 내가 안아 줬잖아, 바로 저 문 안에서. 넌 내내 울어 댔고.
모린: 아빠가 그 얘기를 백 번은 하셨어요. 제가 '항만 노동자 폐활량'을 가졌다고요.
에디: 정말 유감이다, 얘야. 좋은 사람이었어. 우리 중 최고였지.
모린: 감사해요. 와 주셔서도 감사해요. 정말 큰 의미예요. 아빠가 늘 아저씨 얘기를 하셨거든요.
에스포지토 부인: 삼가 조의를 표해요, 모린. 난 돌로레스고, 에디네 건물 한 층 아래 살아요.
모린: 감사해요, 돌로레스. 오늘 두 분 다 와 주셔서 감사해요.
소니: 에디 오닐, 아직 서 있네. 솔직히 다음은 너일 줄 알았는데.
에디: 소니 루소! 이게 누구야. 여든여덟인데 아직도 사람들 욕하고 다니네.
소니: 뭐, 누군가는 해야지. 이제 지미가 없으니까.
모린: 소니 아저씨도 아빠랑 같이 일하셨죠? 9번 부두였죠?
소니: 9번 부두, 1958년부터. 둘보다 내가 먼저 있었으니까, 부둣가에서 제일 오래된 화석이지.
에디: 소니가 지미를 가르쳤고, 지미가 나를 가르쳤어. 족보 같은 거지. 욕이 좀 더 많을 뿐.
모린: 에디, 하나 여쭤봐도 돼요? 좀 큰 부탁인데요.
에디: 뭐든지, 얘야. 말만 해.
모린: 오늘 오후에 더 훅에서 작은 모임을 해요. 아빠에 대해 몇 마디 해 주실래요?
에디: 내가? 연설을? 모린, 난 연설 못 해. 한 번도 잘한 적 없어.
모린: 잘하실 필요 없어요. 진심이기만 하면 돼요. 아빠를 누구보다 오래 아셨잖아요. 소니 아저씨 빼고요.
소니: 날 보지 마. 내가 연설하면 사람들이 울어. 그것도 엉뚱한 이유로.
에디: 알았어. 최선을 다해 볼게. 지미를 위해서 할게.
모린: 감사해요. 전화를 더 자주 드렸어야 했다는 생각만 들어요. 저는 매사피쿼에 살아서 한 달에 한 번밖에 못 왔거든요.
에스포지토 부인: 아, 그렇게 자책하지 말아요. 아무한테도 도움이 안 돼요.
모린: 근데 이틀 동안 혼자 계셨어요, 돌로레스. 이틀이요. 우편물이 쌓이는 걸 우체부가 알아챌 때까지요.
에디: 알아. 들었어. 그 생각이 머리에서 떠나질 않아.
모린: 이사를 안 하시려고 했어요. 열 번은 여쭤봤어요. "우리랑 같이 살아요, 아빠." 그럼 늘 "내 동네는 여기야"라고 하셨어요.
에디: 그게 지미지. 녹슨 볼트처럼 고집이 셌어.
소니: 우리가 다 그래, 늙은 부둣가 쥐들은. 동네를 안 떠나. 실려 나가지.
에스포지토 부인: 소니! 하필 오늘 그런 말을.
소니: 미안. 미안하다, 모린. 슬프면 말이 많아져.
모린: 괜찮아요, 정말로. 아빠라면 웃으셨을 거예요. "소니, 입 다물고 맥주나 사"라고 하셨겠죠.
소니: 60년 동안 매일 그 말을 했는데, 내가 사 준 건 아마 열 잔쯤일 거야.
