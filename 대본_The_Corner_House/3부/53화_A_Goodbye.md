# THE CORNER HOUSE — 3부 53화

## A Goodbye (작별)

> **표기 전용 정보 (낭독하지 않음)**
> - 부/화: 3부 17화(통산 53화) · 시기: 2026년 11월 18일(수) · 난이도: B1~B2 · 턴 수: 120
> - 장소: 비지테이션 성당 앞 계단 → 더 훅(장례 후 모임) → 2층과 3층 사이 계단
> - 등장인물: EDDIE, MRS. ESPOSITO, MAUREEN, SONNY, TOMMY, TASHA
> - 사건: 에디의 부두 시절 선배 지미 도일(86)이 세상을 떠났다. 커피 스트리트에서 혼자 살던 지미는 이틀 뒤에야 우체부에게 발견됐다. 장례 미사 뒤 딸 모린이 에디에게 추도사를 부탁한다. 오후에 더 훅에서 아일랜드식 추모 모임이 열린다. 에디는 1968년 첫 출근 날 지미가 갈고리 쓰는 법을 가르쳐 준 이야기, 컨테이너선이 들어와 부두 일이 뉴저지로 떠나던 시절 이야기를 한다. 밤에는 '혼자 죽는 것'이 두려운 에디에게 부인이 약속을 제안한다. 매일 아침 8시 바닥을 두 번 두드리기.
> - 학습 포인트: 애도 표현(I'm so sorry for your loss / He'll be missed / My condolences / Thank you for coming), 추도사(He was the kind of man who… / I'll never forget… / If Jimmy were here, he'd say…), 조용한 어조와 회상
> - 미국 현안: 혼자 사는 노인의 고독사, 장례 비용, 컨테이너화 이후 사라진 브루클린 부두 일자리, 고령화하는 동네
> - 연결: 대니 월시도 커피 스트리트 출신(15화). 에디의 부두 = 지금의 이케아 자리(26화). 에디는 11화 이후 스마트폰 사용. 43화 레빈 박사·클립보드. 부인은 2B, 에디는 바로 위 3A.

## 대본

[Scene: Wednesday, 11:15 a.m. The steps outside Visitation Church on Verona Street. Cold wind. People in dark coats come out after the funeral Mass.]

MRS. ESPOSITO: Eddie. Here. Take my arm. The steps are slippery.
EDDIE: I'm fine, Dolores. I've walked down these steps for fifty years.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Then walk down them for fifty more. Slowly.
MAUREEN: Mr. O'Neill? Eddie? I'm Maureen. Jimmy's daughter.
EDDIE: Maureen. I know. I held you at your christening. Right inside that door. You screamed the whole time.
MAUREEN: Dad told me. He said I had "longshoreman lungs."
EDDIE: I'm so sorry for your loss, sweetheart. He was a good man. The best.
MAUREEN: Thank you. Thank you for coming. It means a lot. He talked about you all the time.
MRS. ESPOSITO: My condolences, Maureen. I'm Dolores. I live in Eddie's building.
MAUREEN: Thank you, Dolores. Thank you both.
SONNY: Eddie O'Neill. Still standing. I thought you'd be next.
EDDIE: Sonny Russo! Look at you. Eighty-eight and still insulting people.
SONNY: Somebody has to do it. Jimmy's not here anymore.
MAUREEN: Sonny worked with Dad too. Pier Nine. Right?
SONNY: Pier Nine. Nineteen fifty-eight. I was there before both of them. I'm the oldest fossil on the waterfront.
EDDIE: Sonny taught Jimmy. And Jimmy taught me. It's like a family tree. With more bad words.
MAUREEN: Eddie, can I ask you something? A big favor?
EDDIE: Anything.
MAUREEN: We're having a little gathering. At The Hook. This afternoon. Would you say a few words? About Dad?
EDDIE: Me? A speech? Maureen, I'm not good with speeches.
MAUREEN: You don't need to be good. You need to be true. You knew him longer than anyone. Except Sonny.
SONNY: Don't look at me. My speeches make people cry. The wrong kind of crying.
EDDIE: Okay. I'll try. For Jimmy.
MAUREEN: Thank you. I keep thinking... I should have called him more. I lived in Massapequa. I came once a month.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Don't do that to yourself, dear.
MAUREEN: He was alone for two days. Two days. Before the mail carrier noticed. The mail was piling up.
EDDIE: I know. I heard.
MAUREEN: He didn't want to move. I asked him. Ten times. "Come live with us, Dad." He always said, "My street is here."
EDDIE: That was Jimmy. Stubborn as a rusty bolt.
SONNY: We're all like that. The old dock rats. We don't leave. We get carried out.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Sonny! Not today.
SONNY: Sorry. I'm sorry, Maureen. I talk too much when I'm sad.
MAUREEN: It's okay. Dad would laugh at that. He'd say, "Sonny, shut your mouth and buy me a beer."
SONNY: He said that every day for sixty years. I bought maybe ten beers.

[Scene: 2:00 p.m. The Hook. A photo of Jimmy in his work jacket stands on the bar. About forty people. Tommy taps a glass.]

TOMMY: Okay, everybody. Can I have your attention? Thank you for coming. Jimmy Doyle drank here for twenty years.
TASHA: Every Tuesday. Two beers. Never three. And he always left a dollar under the coaster.
TOMMY: Maureen asked Eddie to say a few words. Eddie?
EDDIE: Thank you, Tommy. Okay. I wrote some notes. On a napkin. Tasha's napkin.
TASHA: Take your time, Eddie.
EDDIE: Jimmy Doyle was the kind of man who noticed things. If your boots were wrong, he noticed. If your heart was wrong, he noticed that too.
EDDIE: I met him in nineteen sixty-eight. I had just come home from Vietnam. I had no job. I didn't sleep. I was angry at everybody.
EDDIE: My father knew Jimmy's father. So one morning, I went to the pier. Jimmy looked at me and said, "You're the soldier. Can you lift?" I said yes. He said, "Then lift."
SONNY: That's Jimmy. Two words. Then lift.
EDDIE: That first day, he showed me how to use the hook. The longshoreman's hook. For the coffee sacks. From Brazil.
EDDIE: He said, "Don't fight the sack. Let it sit on your back. Let your legs do the work." I'll never forget that.
EDDIE: Later, I understood. He wasn't only talking about coffee. He was talking about everything. Grief. Bills. Life. Let your legs do the work.
MAUREEN: He said that to me too. When I had my first baby.
EDDIE: Then, in the seventies, the containers came. Big steel boxes. One crane did the work of fifty men. The ships went to New Jersey. To Port Newark. The jobs went with them.
SONNY: Thousands of men. Gone. In ten years.
EDDIE: A lot of guys gave up. Some drank. Some left. Jimmy didn't give up. He fought for every hour. For every man. He went to every union meeting. With a thermos. And a loud voice.
TASHA: He still had that voice. On Tuesdays.
EDDIE: When my Peggy died, I didn't leave my apartment for a week. Jimmy came every day. He didn't say much. He sat on my couch. He watched hockey. He made terrible tea.
TOMMY: Worse than my coffee?
EDDIE: Close, Tommy. Very close.
EDDIE: On the seventh day, he stood up. He said, "Eddie. Let your legs do the work." And he took me to the bar. To this bar.
EDDIE: If Jimmy were here today, he'd say, "Stop crying. Drink something. And somebody call my daughter. She worries."
MAUREEN: That's exactly what he'd say.
EDDIE: Jimmy, you were the kind of friend a man gets once. If he's lucky. I was lucky. Thank you. Rest now. Let somebody else do the lifting.
TOMMY: To Jimmy Doyle.
TASHA: To Jimmy.
SONNY: To Jimmy. You owe me fifty beers, you old goat.
MAUREEN: Eddie, that was beautiful. Thank you. I didn't know half of those stories.
EDDIE: He didn't talk about himself. That was his only fault. That, and the tea.
MAUREEN: Can I tell you something? The funeral home costs twelve thousand dollars. Twelve thousand. For three days.
TASHA: Twelve thousand? For a funeral?
MAUREEN: That's the simple one. Dad had a little insurance from the union. It covered half. My husband and I paid the rest.
SONNY: When I go, put me in a coffee sack. Throw me in the harbor. Free.
TOMMY: That's illegal, Sonny.
SONNY: So arrest me. I'll be dead.
TOMMY: Maureen, the bar is paying for today. The food. The drinks. All of it.
MAUREEN: Tommy, no. You don't have to—
TOMMY: Jimmy left a dollar under that coaster every Tuesday for twenty years. That's about a thousand dollars. I'm just paying him back.
MAUREEN: Thank you. Dad loved this place. He said it was the last real bar in Brooklyn.
TASHA: He said that to everybody. Every Tuesday.
MAUREEN: Eddie, Dad left something for you. In his will.
EDDIE: For me? Jimmy had a will?
MAUREEN: One page. Handwritten. He left you his hook. The old one. From Pier Nine.
EDDIE: His hook. He kept it? All these years?
MAUREEN: He wrote, "For Eddie. He'll know what to do with it."
EDDIE: I'll hang it over my door. Next to Peggy's picture.
SONNY: And me? Did he leave me anything?
MAUREEN: He left you his bar tab, Sonny.
TOMMY: Now everybody, one song. Jimmy's song. Sonny, you know it.
SONNY: "The Parting Glass." I know it. My voice is terrible. But I know it.
EDDIE: Sing it, Sonny. Terrible is fine. Jimmy sang it terrible too.
SONNY: Okay. Everybody help me. Slowly. Like it's the last glass of the night.

[Scene: 9:30 p.m. The staircase between the second and third floors. Eddie sits on a step. Mrs. Esposito comes out of 2B in her robe.]

MRS. ESPOSITO: Eddie? What are you doing on the stairs? It's cold.
EDDIE: Resting. The second floor is my halfway house. Dr. Levine says rest on the way.
MRS. ESPOSITO: You've been sitting here for twenty minutes. I heard you through the door.
EDDIE: Okay. I'm not only resting. I'm thinking.
MRS. ESPOSITO: About Jimmy?
EDDIE: About the two days. Jimmy on his kitchen floor. For two days. And nobody knew.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Oh, Eddie.
EDDIE: I'm not afraid of dying, Dolores. I'm eighty-three. I made my peace with that. But I'm afraid of that. The two days. Alone. With the mail piling up.
MRS. ESPOSITO: I understand. I think about it too. I live alone too. With a cat who doesn't call anyone.
EDDIE: Biscuit would eat me before she called anyone.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Probably. She's practical.
EDDIE: Jimmy's hook is in my coat pocket. Maureen gave it to me.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Can I see it? Oh. It's heavy. And still sharp.
EDDIE: Michael says, "Move to Florida, Dad." Maybe he's right. Maybe I'm being stubborn. Like Jimmy.
MRS. ESPOSITO: You're being Eddie. That's different. But listen. I have an idea.
EDDIE: Of course you do. You always have an idea. And a clipboard.
MRS. ESPOSITO: No clipboard. This one is simple. Every morning, at eight o'clock, you knock on your floor. Twice. With your cane.
EDDIE: My floor is your ceiling.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Exactly. I hear two knocks, I know you're alive. Then I knock back with my broom. Twice. So you know I'm alive.
EDDIE: And if there's no knock?
MRS. ESPOSITO: Then I come upstairs. I have your spare key. Since two thousand twelve. Since Sandy.
EDDIE: I forgot you had that.
MRS. ESPOSITO: I didn't forget. I never forget a key.
EDDIE: Eight o'clock. Two knocks. What if I oversleep?
MRS. ESPOSITO: You haven't overslept since nineteen seventy-four. You wake up at six. I hear your radio.
EDDIE: You hear my radio? Every morning?
MRS. ESPOSITO: Every morning. The weather. Then the Irish music on Sundays. I like it, actually.
EDDIE: You never told me.
MRS. ESPOSITO: You never asked. Now, are you going to sit here all night? Or are you going to let your legs do the work?
EDDIE: You were listening. At the bar.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Everybody was listening, Eddie. It was a good speech. Jimmy would be proud.
EDDIE: Okay. Help me up. Slowly. Tomorrow, eight o'clock. Two knocks.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Two knocks. And Eddie? Two knocks back. Every day. As long as we're both here.
EDDIE: As long as we're both here. That's a deal, Dolores.

## 표현 정리 (낭독하지 않음)

- **애도 표현**
  - *I'm so sorry for your loss.* — 가장 기본적인 조의 표현. 유족에게.
  - *My condolences.* — "삼가 조의를 표합니다." 조금 더 격식 있다.
  - *He'll be missed. / He was a good man.* — 고인을 기릴 때.
  - *Thank you for coming. It means a lot.* — 유족의 답례.
  - *Don't do that to yourself.* — "자책하지 마."
- **추도사 표현**
  - *He was the kind of man who noticed things.* — "그는 ~하는 사람이었다." 고인의 성품을 한 문장으로.
  - *I'll never forget that.* — "그건 절대 잊지 못할 겁니다."
  - *If Jimmy were here today, he'd say…* — 가정법 과거로 고인의 목소리를 불러온다.
  - *Rest now.* — "이제 편히 쉬어요."
  - *To Jimmy Doyle.* — 건배사. "지미 도일을 위하여."
- **longshoreman** — 항만 노동자. *Pier Nine* 9번 부두. *dock rats* 부둣가 사람들(자조적 별명).
- **Stubborn as a rusty bolt.** — "녹슨 볼트처럼 고집 세다."
- **Let your legs do the work.** — "다리가 일하게 해." 이 화의 핵심 문장. 힘든 것과 싸우지 말고 버티는 법.
- **made my peace with** — ~을 받아들였다.
- **As long as we're both here.** — "우리 둘 다 여기 있는 한."
- **you old goat** — "이 늙은 염소야." 오랜 친구 사이의 애정 섞인 욕. 친한 사이에서만, 웃으면서.

## 낭독 포인트 (낭독하지 않음)

- 이 화는 전체적으로 작은 음량, 느린 템포로 읽는다.
- **I'm so sorry for your loss.** — *so*에 강세, 끝은 내려서 조용히. 빠르게 말하면 형식적으로 들린다.
- 에디의 추도사는 한 문장마다 쉰다. *He said, "Then lift."* 뒤에서 2초 쉼.
- **Let your legs do the work.** — 추도사에서 세 번 나온다. 매번 조금씩 더 천천히.
- 소니는 거칠고 빠르게, 그러나 *Thousands of men. Gone.*에서는 목소리를 떨어뜨린다.
- 마지막 장면은 속삭이듯. *Two knocks.*를 말할 때 손가락으로 두 번 두드리는 리듬을 상상한다.

## 한글 해석

에스포지토 부인: 에디. 여기요. 내 팔 잡아요. 계단 미끄러워요.
에디: 괜찮아, 돌로레스. 이 계단을 50년 동안 걸어 내려갔어.
에스포지토 부인: 그럼 50년 더 걸어 내려가요. 천천히.
모린: 오닐 씨? 에디 아저씨? 저 모린이에요. 지미 딸이요.
에디: 모린. 알지. 네 세례식 때 내가 안아 줬어. 바로 저 문 안에서. 내내 울어 댔지.
모린: 아빠가 말해 줬어요. 제가 '항만 노동자 폐활량'을 가졌다고요.
에디: 정말 안타깝구나, 얘야. 좋은 사람이었어. 최고였지.
모린: 감사해요. 와 주셔서 감사해요. 큰 힘이 돼요. 아빠가 아저씨 얘기를 늘 했어요.
에스포지토 부인: 삼가 조의를 표해요, 모린. 난 돌로레스예요. 에디랑 같은 건물에 살아요.
모린: 감사해요, 돌로레스. 두 분 다 감사해요.
소니: 에디 오닐. 아직 서 있네. 다음은 너일 줄 알았는데.
에디: 소니 루소! 이게 누구야. 여든여덟에 아직도 사람 놀리고 다니네.
소니: 누군가는 해야지. 이제 지미가 없잖아.
모린: 소니 아저씨도 아빠랑 같이 일했어요. 9번 부두. 맞죠?
소니: 9번 부두. 1958년. 내가 둘보다 먼저 있었지. 부둣가에서 제일 오래된 화석이야.
에디: 소니가 지미를 가르쳤고. 지미가 나를 가르쳤어. 족보 같은 거야. 욕이 좀 더 많은.
모린: 에디 아저씨, 뭐 하나 부탁해도 돼요? 큰 부탁이요?
에디: 뭐든지.
모린: 작은 모임을 해요. 더 훅에서요. 오늘 오후에요. 아빠에 대해 몇 마디 해 주실래요?
에디: 내가? 연설을? 모린, 난 연설 못 해.
모린: 잘하실 필요 없어요. 진심이면 돼요. 아저씨가 아빠를 제일 오래 아셨잖아요. 소니 아저씨 빼고요.
소니: 날 보지 마. 내 연설은 사람들을 울려. 잘못된 쪽으로.
에디: 그래. 해 볼게. 지미를 위해서.
모린: 감사해요. 자꾸 생각나요… 전화를 더 자주 했어야 했는데. 매사피쿼에 살아서요. 한 달에 한 번 왔어요.
에스포지토 부인: 자책하지 말아요, 얘야.
모린: 아빠가 이틀 동안 혼자였어요. 이틀이요. 우체부가 알아챌 때까지요. 우편물이 쌓여 있었대요.
에디: 알아. 들었어.
모린: 이사를 안 하려고 하셨어요. 제가 물어봤어요. 열 번이요. "아빠, 우리랑 같이 살아요." 늘 "내 거리는 여기야"라고 하셨어요.
에디: 그게 지미지. 녹슨 볼트처럼 고집이 셌어.
소니: 우리 다 그래. 늙은 부둣가 쥐들. 우린 안 떠나. 실려 나가지.
에스포지토 부인: 소니! 오늘은 그런 말 말아요.
소니: 미안. 미안해, 모린. 슬프면 말이 많아져.
모린: 괜찮아요. 아빠라면 웃었을 거예요. "소니, 입 다물고 맥주나 사"라고 했겠죠.
소니: 60년 동안 매일 그 말을 했어. 내가 산 건 한 열 잔쯤이야.
토미: 자, 여러분. 잠깐 주목해 주실래요? 와 주셔서 감사합니다. 지미 도일은 20년 동안 여기서 술을 마셨어요.
타샤: 매주 화요일이요. 맥주 두 잔. 세 잔은 절대 안 마셨어요. 그리고 늘 컵받침 밑에 1달러를 두고 갔어요.
토미: 모린이 에디에게 몇 마디 부탁했어요. 에디?
에디: 고마워, 토미. 자. 메모를 좀 했어. 냅킨에. 타샤 냅킨에.
타샤: 천천히 하세요, 에디.
에디: 지미 도일은 뭐든 알아차리는 사람이었어요. 장화가 잘못됐으면 알아차렸죠. 마음이 잘못됐으면 그것도 알아차렸어요.
에디: 그를 만난 건 1968년이었어요. 베트남에서 막 돌아왔을 때죠. 일자리가 없었어요. 잠도 못 잤어요. 모든 사람한테 화가 나 있었어요.
에디: 우리 아버지가 지미 아버지를 알았어요. 그래서 어느 날 아침 부두에 갔죠. 지미가 날 보더니 "네가 그 군인이구나. 들 수 있어?"라고 했어요. 그렇다고 했죠. 그러자 "그럼 들어"라고 했어요.
소니: 그게 지미야. 두 마디. 그럼 들어.
