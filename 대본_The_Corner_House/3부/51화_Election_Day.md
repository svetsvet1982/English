# THE CORNER HOUSE — 3부 51화

## Election Day (선거일)

> **표기 전용 정보 (낭독하지 않음)**
> - 부/화: 3부 15화(통산 51화) · 시기: 2026년 11월 3일(화) 아침·밤 · 난이도: B1~B2 · 턴 수: 120
> - 장소: 동네 초등학교 체육관 투표소 → 더 훅
> - 등장인물: MRS. ESPOSITO, EDDIE, JESSE, MARISOL, DIEGO, TOMMY, FRANKIE, MARCUS
> - 사건: 중간선거일. 에스포지토 부인은 1994년부터 투표소 선거 사무원이다. 에디는 1964년 이후 한 번도 투표를 빠뜨리지 않았다. 실직한 제시는 처음으로 중간선거에 투표한다. 밤에는 더 훅에서 개표 방송을 보며 가상의 주 하원 후보 두 사람(애나 루이스, 폴 도널리)을 두고 마리솔과 사촌 프랭키가 '정중하게' 맞선다. 토미의 바 규칙: "사람 말고 의견을 놓고 싸워라."
> - 학습 포인트: 선거 어휘(ballot, polling site, poll worker, turnout, candidate, early voting, mail-in ballot, too close to call), 정중한 반대 화법(I see your point, but… / Fair enough. / Let's agree to disagree. / With respect, …)
> - 미국 현안: 2026년 중간선거, 생활비·임대료·소상공인 부담·홍수 대비·치안 등 동네의 쟁점, 투표하지 못하는 영주권자, 개표가 늦어지는 박빙 선거 (실제 정당명·정치인 언급 없음)
> - 연결: 아르투로(30년 영주권자)가 드디어 시민권 신청서를 작성했다(61화 복선). 프랭키는 42화에서 베팅 앱을 지웠다. 제시는 46화에서 실직했다.

## 대본

[Scene: Tuesday, 7:10 a.m. The gym of the elementary school on Sullivan Street. Folding tables, flags, and a long line. Mrs. Esposito sits behind a table with a badge.]

MRS. ESPOSITO: Good morning! Next, please. Name and address?
EDDIE: Edward O'Neill, Van Brunt Street. You know my address, Dolores, because you live right under me.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Rules are rules, Eddie. Today I'm a poll worker, not your neighbor.
EDDIE: Since when are you not my neighbor?
MRS. ESPOSITO: Since six a.m. this morning, and until nine p.m. tonight. Since nineteen ninety-four, actually, if you count every election.
EDDIE: Thirty-two years at the same folding table. They should give you a medal.
MRS. ESPOSITO: They give me two hundred and fifty dollars and a cold sandwich. Sign here, please.
EDDIE: There. My signature looks worse every year, so I hope they still believe it's me.
MRS. ESPOSITO: It looks like a heartbeat, and that's good enough. Here's your ballot, and here's a privacy sleeve.
DIEGO: Mrs. Esposito! Do you work here?
MRS. ESPOSITO: Diego! What are you doing here? Is there no school today?
DIEGO: Schools are closed on Election Day, because the gyms are used for voting.
MARISOL: And because the teachers need a break, too. Good morning, Dolores.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Good morning, Marisol. Go to the table on the left, because your election district is over there.
DIEGO: Mom, can I fill in the bubbles for you? I'm really good at staying inside the lines.
MARISOL: No, mijo. Only I can touch my ballot, because that's the law.
DIEGO: Then can I at least watch you do it?
MARISOL: You can stand next to me, but you can't look. The vote is secret.
JESSE: Hey, everybody. Is this the right line? I've never voted at this polling site before.
EDDIE: Jesse! You're up early for an unemployed man.
JESSE: I'm unemployed, not dead. And I wanted to vote before my brain wakes up and gets nervous.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Name, please?
JESSE: Jesse Moore. I just moved to Gowanus a few months ago. Am I still at this site?
MRS. ESPOSITO: Let me check. It says you updated your address in August, so your new site is on Third Avenue.
JESSE: Wait, seriously? I'm at the wrong place? I've been standing in this line for twenty minutes.
MRS. ESPOSITO: Don't panic. You can vote there, and it's only a fifteen-minute walk. Or you can fill out an affidavit ballot here.
JESSE: What's an affidavit ballot? It sounds like something from a courtroom.
MRS. ESPOSITO: It's a paper ballot that goes in an envelope, and they check it later. But your real site is safer, because it counts tonight.
JESSE: Then I'll walk. Thanks, Mrs. E. You just saved my vote.
EDDIE: Wait a second, kid. Is this your first time voting?
JESSE: It's my first midterm. I voted for president twice, but the elections in the middle? I always skipped those, because I thought they didn't matter.
EDDIE: The small ones are the main show, though. The small ones decide your rent, your schools, and your streets.
EDDIE: My first vote was in nineteen sixty-four, when I was twenty-one. My father walked me to the booth himself.
EDDIE: I haven't missed an election since then. Not one, not even the one when I had the hernia.
JESSE: Okay, no pressure at all. Sixty-two years of perfect attendance, and I can't even find the right gym.
MARISOL: Jesse, don't forget to turn the ballot over, because there are proposals on the back.
JESSE: There's a back? Who designs these things?
MARISOL: Read them before you go in. They're about the city, not just about the candidates.
DIEGO: Mom, why isn't Abuelo here? He always says that the news is important.
MARISOL: Abuelo can't vote, mijo. He's a permanent resident, so he isn't a citizen yet.
DIEGO: But he's lived here forever! It's been forty-two years!
MARISOL: I know. But guess what? Last week, he finally filled out the citizenship form.
DIEGO: Really? So Abuelo is going to be American?
MARISOL: He already is, in his heart. Now he wants the paper too. He said, "I want to vote before I die."
MRS. ESPOSITO: Tell him I'll be here when he does. I might be ninety by then, but I'll be right here at this table.
EDDIE: Okay, I'm going in. Wish me luck in there.
DIEGO: Good luck! Pick the good one!
EDDIE: I always pick the good one. The problem is that the other people pick the other good one.

[Scene: 9:40 p.m. The Hook. The TV shows results. The bar is full and loud.]

TOMMY: Okay, everybody! Quiet, please! The local results are coming on right now!
FRANKIE: Turn it up, Tommy! I want to hear Donnelly win!
MARISOL: And I want to hear Ruiz win. So turn it up for both of us.
TOMMY: Before anybody says anything, there's the rule. You all know the rule in this bar.
MARCUS: Argue about ideas. Not about people.
TOMMY: Thank you, Marcus. And nobody throws anything, except money. At me.
FRANKIE: Look at the numbers for State Assembly. Donnelly has fifty-one percent, and Ruiz has forty-nine.
MARISOL: That's with only thirty percent counted. It's still early, Frankie.
FRANKIE: Sure, it's early, but I like what I see.
JESSE: I voted! I walked all the way to Third Avenue, and I got the sticker. Look!
MARCUS: Nice. They said on the news that turnout is high this year, higher than the last midterm.
TOMMY: Good. Everybody's angry about something, and angry people vote.
FRANKIE: Can I say why I like Donnelly? Politely, I promise.
TOMMY: Politely. You get one minute.
FRANKIE: I run a small business, painting, with three guys and a van. Every year, there are more fees, more permits, and more taxes.
MARISOL: That's fair. I won't argue with that part.
FRANKIE: And then there's the van. Every time I go into Manhattan, I pay the toll, and that's a hundred dollars a week. Donnelly says he'll fight for small guys like me.
MARISOL: I see your point. I really do. Small businesses are struggling, and you only have to look at Tommy.
TOMMY: Leave me out of this, please. I'm just the referee tonight.
FRANKIE: And there's safety, too. My cousin's store got broken into twice, so Donnelly wants more cops on the street.
MARCUS: With respect, Frankie, my bike was stolen too. It happened twice. But more cops didn't bring them back.
FRANKIE: Okay. Fair enough. But more cops doesn't hurt, does it?
MARCUS: Maybe it does, maybe it doesn't. I'm still thinking about it.
TOMMY: Marisol, it's your turn now. You get one minute, too.
MARISOL: Thank you. I like Ruiz because of rent. Half this building is fighting a landlord right now, and half this neighborhood is scared.
FRANKIE: Okay, I get that.
MARISOL: Ruiz supports a law that gives tenants the first chance to buy their building, before a big company can buy it.
FRANKIE: Tenants buying buildings? With what money? Nobody here has that kind of money.
MARISOL: With help, like loans and a special fund. It already works in other cities.
FRANKIE: Come on, Marisol. That sounds like a dream to me.
MARISOL: With respect, Frankie, a lot of things sounded like dreams before they actually happened.
FRANKIE: Okay, I hear you. But who pays for the fund? It's guys like me, with more taxes.
MARISOL: Maybe. Or maybe the big companies pay for it, the ones that buy buildings and empty them.
JESSE: Can I add one thing? Ruiz also wants the flood walls finished faster.
FRANKIE: Okay, see, I agree with that one. My basement flooded again last week, and I'm still drying out the boxes.
MARISOL: You agree with something! Write it down, Tommy, so he can't take it back!
TOMMY: Noted. Frankie and Marisol agree on water, so we're making history tonight.
MARCUS: Look, there are new numbers. With sixty percent counted, it's Ruiz at fifty percent and Donnelly at fifty percent.
FRANKIE: Fifty-fifty? You're kidding me.
TOMMY: Now it's a real game. It's like the Knicks in the fourth quarter.
FRANKIE: Don't talk to me about the fourth quarter. I deleted the app, remember?
TOMMY: I remember, and I'm proud of you for that.
JESSE: So who are you voting for, Marcus? Or did you already vote?
MARCUS: I voted early, on Saturday. There was no line, and it took five minutes. Early voting is the best-kept secret in Brooklyn.
FRANKIE: And? Who did you pick?
MARCUS: That's between me and the ballot, my friend.
FRANKIE: Come on! Just tell me! I won't tell anybody!
MARCUS: I'll tell you this much. I picked people from each side, for different races.
MARISOL: A split ticket! Marcus, you're a total mystery.
MARCUS: I'm not a mystery. I'm a small business owner and a tenant, so basically, I'm both of you at the same time.
TOMMY: That's the smartest thing anybody has said in this bar tonight.
FRANKIE: Okay, it's seventy-five percent counted now, and Donnelly is up. He's ahead by three hundred votes.
MARISOL: Three hundred votes is nothing, Frankie. That's like two apartment buildings.
MARCUS: And there are still mail-in ballots. They count those later, so it could take days.
TOMMY: Look, the TV is saying it now. "Too close to call."
FRANKIE: Too close to call. So we all go home, and we still don't know.
MARISOL: That's democracy, Frankie. Sometimes you just have to wait.
FRANKIE: I hate waiting. I really hate it.
MARISOL: Me too. So that's one more thing we agree on.

[Scene: 11:30 p.m. The bar is almost empty. Frankie puts on his jacket.]

FRANKIE: Marisol, before I go. No hard feelings, right?
MARISOL: No hard feelings. Let's agree to disagree. On most things, anyway.
FRANKIE: On most things. But not on the water, and not on Tommy's coffee.
TOMMY: Hey! The coffee is not on the ballot.
MARISOL: It should be, though. Proposal number three: "Fix the coffee."
FRANKIE: I'd vote yes on that one. And I never vote yes on anything.
MARCUS: See? Red Hook is finally united, against one cup of coffee.
TOMMY: Get out of my bar, all of you. Thank you for voting, and now go to bed.
FRANKIE: Night, cousin. Hey, if Ruiz wins, will you tell me about this tenant fund thing? For real?
MARISOL: For real. I'll tell you everything, so bring your questions. And bring your angry face.
TOMMY: Thanks, everybody. Even with all the arguing, this was a good night.
JESSE: Nobody won anything. But it was still a good night.
TOMMY: That's a neighborhood, Jesse. Nobody wins. Everybody shows up.

## 표현 정리 (낭독하지 않음)

- **선거 어휘**
  - *ballot* 투표용지 / *polling site* 투표소 / *poll worker* 선거 사무원 / *election district* 선거구
  - *privacy sleeve* 투표용지 가림 폴더 / *affidavit ballot* 선서 투표용지(명부에 문제가 있을 때 쓰는 임시 투표, 나중에 확인)
  - *early voting* 사전 투표 / *mail-in ballot* 우편 투표 / *turnout* 투표율 / *candidate* 후보 / *proposal* (투표용지 뒷면의) 주민 발의안
  - *split ticket* 선거마다 다른 쪽 후보를 고르는 교차 투표 / *too close to call* 박빙이라 승자를 예측할 수 없음
- **정중한 반대 화법**
  - *I see your point. I really do.* — 상대 말을 먼저 인정한다.
  - *With respect, …* — "외람되지만…" 반대 의견 앞에 붙인다.
  - *Fair enough.* — "그건 일리 있네."
  - *I hear you. But…* — "무슨 말인지 알아. 그런데…"
  - *Let's agree to disagree.* — "서로 생각이 다르다는 걸 인정하자."
  - *No hard feelings, right?* — "기분 나쁜 거 없지?"
- **That's between me and the ballot.** — "그건 나랑 투표용지만 아는 거야." 지지 후보를 밝히지 않을 때.
- **I'm unemployed, not dead.** — "실직했지, 죽은 건 아니야."
- **perfect attendance** — 개근.
- **Turn it up.** — 소리 좀 키워.

## 낭독 포인트 (낭독하지 않음)

- 후보 이름 강세: **RU**-iz [루이스, 첫음절], **DON**-nel-ly [다널리, 첫음절].
- 숫자는 또렷하게: *fifty-one percent* [핍티원 퍼센트], *three hundred votes*.
- **With respect, | Frankie, | …** — 쉼표마다 쉬며 부드럽게. 반대하는 말일수록 목소리를 낮추고 천천히 읽는다.
- *I see your point.*는 *point*를 올리고, 뒤의 *I really do.*로 진심을 실어 내려 준다.
- 부인은 선거 사무원답게 사무적인 톤: *Name and address? / Sign here, please.*
- 마지막 토미의 *Nobody wins. | Everybody shows up.* — 두 문장을 대칭으로, 천천히.

## 한글 해석

에스포지토 부인: 안녕하세요! 다음 분이요. 성함이랑 주소요?
에디: 에드워드 오닐, 밴 브런트 스트리트. 내 주소 알잖아, 돌로레스. 바로 내 아래층에 살면서.
에스포지토 부인: 규칙은 규칙이에요, 에디. 오늘 난 이웃이 아니라 선거 사무원이에요.
에디: 언제부터 내 이웃이 아니었는데?
에스포지토 부인: 오늘 아침 6시부터 밤 9시까지요. 선거를 다 따지면 사실 1994년부터죠.
에디: 같은 접이식 테이블에서 32년이네. 훈장을 줘야겠어.
에스포지토 부인: 250달러랑 차가운 샌드위치를 줘요. 여기 서명하세요.
에디: 됐다. 내 서명은 해마다 더 엉망이야. 아직 나라고 믿어 주면 좋겠네.
에스포지토 부인: 심전도처럼 생겼네요. 그거면 충분해요. 투표용지 여기요. 그리고 가림 폴더도요.
디에고: 에스포지토 할머니! 여기서 일하세요?
에스포지토 부인: 디에고! 여기서 뭐 하니? 오늘 학교 안 가?
디에고: 선거일엔 학교가 쉬어요. 체육관을 투표하는 데 쓰니까요.
마리솔: 그리고 선생님들도 좀 쉬셔야 하니까요. 좋은 아침이에요, 돌로레스.
에스포지토 부인: 좋은 아침, 마리솔. 왼쪽 테이블로 가요. 마리솔 선거구는 그쪽이에요.
디에고: 엄마, 동그라미 내가 칠해 줘도 돼요? 나 선 안 넘게 진짜 잘 칠해요.
마리솔: 안 돼, 얘야. 엄마 투표용지는 엄마만 만질 수 있어. 그게 법이야.
디에고: 그럼 하는 거 구경이라도 해도 돼요?
마리솔: 옆에 서 있어도 되지만 보면 안 돼. 투표는 비밀이야.
제시: 안녕하세요, 여러분. 여기 줄 맞아요? 이 투표소에서는 투표해 본 적이 없어서요.
에디: 제시! 실업자치고는 일찍 일어났네.
제시: 실직했지, 죽은 건 아니에요. 그리고 뇌가 깨서 긴장하기 전에 투표하고 싶었어요.
에스포지토 부인: 성함이요?
제시: 제시 무어요. 몇 달 전에 고와너스로 이사했어요. 아직 여기서 투표하나요?
에스포지토 부인: 확인해 볼게요. 8월에 주소를 변경했다고 나오네요. 그래서 새 투표소는 3번가예요.
제시: 잠깐만요, 진짜요? 제가 엉뚱한 데 온 거예요? 이 줄에 20분이나 서 있었는데요.
에스포지토 부인: 당황하지 말아요. 거기서 투표하면 되는데, 걸어서 15분밖에 안 걸려요. 아니면 여기서 선서 투표용지를 써도 되고요.
제시: 선서 투표용지가 뭐예요? 법정에서 나오는 말 같은데요.
에스포지토 부인: 봉투에 넣는 종이 투표용지예요. 나중에 확인하죠. 그래도 원래 투표소가 더 안전해요. 오늘 밤에 집계되니까요.
제시: 그럼 걸어갈게요. 고마워요, 부인. 제 표를 살려 주셨어요.
에디: 잠깐만, 젊은이. 이번이 첫 투표야?
제시: 첫 중간선거요. 대선은 두 번 투표했는데, 중간에 있는 선거요? 별로 안 중요한 줄 알고 늘 건너뛰었어요.
에디: 그래도 작은 선거가 본 공연이야. 작은 선거가 네 월세, 학교, 거리를 정하거든.
에디: 내 첫 투표는 스물한 살이던 1964년이었어. 아버지가 직접 투표소까지 데려다줬지.
에디: 그 뒤로 선거를 한 번도 안 빠졌어. 한 번도. 탈장 수술했을 때도.
제시: 좋아요, 부담 하나도 없네요. 62년 개근인데, 전 맞는 체육관도 못 찾아요.
마리솔: 제시, 투표용지 뒤집어 보는 거 잊지 마. 뒷면에 발의안이 있거든.
제시: 뒷면이 있어요? 이거 누가 디자인해요?
마리솔: 들어가기 전에 읽어 봐. 후보만 있는 게 아니라 시에 관한 내용이야.
디에고: 엄마, 할아버지는 왜 안 왔어요? 할아버지는 늘 뉴스가 중요하다고 하잖아요.
마리솔: 할아버지는 투표를 못 하셔, 얘야. 영주권자라서 아직 시민이 아니거든.
디에고: 근데 여기서 평생 사셨잖아요! 42년이나요!
마리솔: 알아. 근데 있잖아. 지난주에 드디어 시민권 신청서를 쓰셨어.
디에고: 진짜요? 그럼 할아버지가 미국 사람이 되는 거예요?
