# THE CORNER HOUSE — 3부 39화

## The Bank Says No (은행이 거절하다)

> **표기 전용 정보 (낭독하지 않음)**
> - 부/화: 3부 3화(통산 39화) · 시기: 2026년 9월, 목요일 오전 · 난이도: B1~B2 · 턴 수: 120
> - 장소: 은행 지점 대기석 → 대출 담당자 사무실 → 은행 밖 거리
> - 등장인물: GINA, TOMMY, MS. CHEN
> - 사건: 토미가 15만 달러 소상공인(SBA) 대출을 신청한다. 담당자 첸은 정중하게 세 가지 걱정을 설명한다. 8개월 뒤 끝나는 상가 계약, 담보 없음, 빠듯한 이익. 여기에 바닥 공사비 1만 1천 달러를 카드로 낸 탓에 신용점수도 떨어졌다. 대출은 거절되지만, 첸은 '비공식적으로' 비영리 지역 대출 기관을 알려 준다.
> - 학습 포인트: 금융 어휘(collateral, credit score, revenue, profit, interest rate, guarantee), 격식 화법(May I be direct? / At this time, we're unable to…), 실망 표현
> - 미국 현안: 고금리 시대의 소상공인 대출, 상가 임대 불안이 대출을 막는 악순환, 지역개발 금융기관(CDFI)
> - 연결: 'Brooklyn Neighborhood Fund'(가상) 연락처 → 54화. 첸의 아버지도 2019년 선셋파크 식당 임대를 잃음. "혼자 싸우지 마세요."

## 대본

[Scene: Thursday, 9:50 a.m. A bank branch on Court Street. Tommy and Gina wait on a bench.]

GINA: Dad, stop moving your leg. You're shaking the whole bench, and people are looking.
TOMMY: I'm nervous. The last time I was in a bank office was two thousand six, when I came to apply for a loan to open the bar.
GINA: And they said yes that time.
TOMMY: They said yes because Sal co-signed. Sal isn't here now.
GINA: But you have twenty years of history now, and that's worth something.
TOMMY: Twenty years of history and a shoebox full of receipts.
GINA: It's not a shoebox anymore, because I put everything in a folder. Look: tax returns, bank statements, and a business plan.
TOMMY: A business plan. I never had a business plan in my life. I had a corner.
GINA: Now you have both. Remember the numbers: a hundred and fifty thousand, for the outdoor seating, the new kitchen equipment, and a cash reserve.
TOMMY: A cash reserve, for the new rent.
GINA: For the new rent. If it goes up, you need six months of savings, and that's what the plan says.
TOMMY: And what if she asks about the lease?
GINA: Tell the truth. It ends in May, the new owner wants more, and we're negotiating.
TOMMY: We're not negotiating, because Whitlock won't even give me a number.
GINA: Then say "the terms are under discussion." That sounds much better.
TOMMY: "The terms are under discussion." You sound exactly like your boss.
GINA: Don't insult me, Dad. I'm here on a sick day.
TOMMY: A sick day? You took a sick day for this?
GINA: I'm sick of my job, so it counts.
TOMMY: Ha, okay. Here she comes, so sit up straight.

[Scene: 10:00 a.m. A small glass office. A woman in her forties with a file.]

MS. CHEN: Mr. Russo? Good morning. I'm Linda Chen. Please come in.
TOMMY: Good morning, Ms. Chen. This is my daughter, Gina, who helped me with the application.
MS. CHEN: Nice to meet you both. Please, have a seat. Can I offer you some water before we start?
GINA: No, thank you. We're fine.
MS. CHEN: Mr. Russo, I've reviewed your application, and thank you for the documents. They're very complete.
TOMMY: That's all Gina, not me.
MS. CHEN: Well, it shows. Let me start by saying that your business has a long history. Twenty years is impressive, because most restaurants close in five.
TOMMY: Thank you. We've survived a lot: Sandy, the pandemic, and the Mets.
MS. CHEN: I'm a Yankees fan, so I can't comment on that last one.
TOMMY: I'll try not to hold it against you.
MS. CHEN: I appreciate that. Now, the request. You're asking for a hundred and fifty thousand dollars through the SBA seven-a program.
TOMMY: That's right.
MS. CHEN: Could you tell me, in your own words, what the loan is for?
TOMMY: Three things. Outdoor seating, so we can grow in the summer. New kitchen equipment, because my fryer is from two thousand nine. And a reserve, in case my rent goes up.
MS. CHEN: Thank you, that's clear. I do have some concerns, though. May I be direct?
TOMMY: Please. Everybody's direct with me lately, so I'm getting used to it.
MS. CHEN: The first concern is the lease. Your lease expires in May, which is only eight months from now.
GINA: The terms are under discussion with the new owner.
MS. CHEN: I understand. But for the bank, it's a serious risk. The loan would be for ten years, and your right to the space is less than one year.
TOMMY: So if they don't renew, I'm stuck with a loan and no bar.
MS. CHEN: Exactly. And the outdoor seating and the equipment both depend on the location.
TOMMY: Okay, so what's the second concern?
MS. CHEN: Collateral. We usually need something to secure the loan, like real estate, for example. Do you own any property?
TOMMY: No. I rent my apartment, which is upstairs in the same building.
MS. CHEN: I see. So the equipment would be the only collateral, and used kitchen equipment doesn't have much value.
GINA: But the SBA guarantees part of the loan, right? Doesn't that help?
MS. CHEN: It helps. For a loan this size, the SBA guarantees up to eighty-five percent. But we still have to show that the business can repay, which brings me to the third concern.
TOMMY: The numbers.
MS. CHEN: The numbers. Your revenue is steady, at about four hundred and fifty thousand a year. But your profit is thin, at around forty-five thousand.
TOMMY: Forty-five thousand. That's my salary, so that's basically me.
MS. CHEN: I understand. Now, at current interest rates, a loan of this size would mean payments of about two thousand a month.
GINA: Two thousand a month, out of a profit of about four thousand a month.
MS. CHEN: That's half of your income. And if your rent increases significantly, the business may not be able to cover both.
TOMMY: So the loan is for the higher rent, but the higher rent makes the loan impossible.
MS. CHEN: I know it sounds circular, and I'm sorry.
TOMMY: What about my credit? Is that a problem too?
MS. CHEN: Your personal credit score is fair, at six eighty. It went down this summer.
TOMMY: That's the floor. I put the new floor on my credit card, and it was eleven thousand dollars.
MS. CHEN: That explains it. The card balance is high compared to the limit.
GINA: He had no choice, because the landlord refused to pay and the floor was rotten.
MS. CHEN: I believe you. Unfortunately, the credit report doesn't know the reasons. It only knows the balance.
TOMMY: So what's the answer, Ms. Chen?
MS. CHEN: I'm sorry, Mr. Russo. At this time, we're unable to approve the loan.
TOMMY: At this time.
MS. CHEN: At this time. If the lease is renewed for five or ten years, please come back, because that would change the picture completely.
TOMMY: But I need the money in order to renew the lease.
MS. CHEN: I understand the problem, Mr. Russo. I really do.
GINA: Is there anything else we can do? Any other option?
MS. CHEN: Can I make a suggestion? It's off the record.
TOMMY: Please.
MS. CHEN: There are nonprofit lenders called community development lenders. They work with small businesses that banks like ours can't help.
GINA: What's the difference between them and a bank?
MS. CHEN: They're more flexible. They look at the history and the community, not only the collateral. Some even have programs for businesses facing displacement.
TOMMY: Displacement. That's a fancy word for "getting kicked out."
MS. CHEN: Yes, it is. There's one in Brooklyn that I know well, so I'll write down the name for you.
GINA: Thank you, Ms. Chen. That's very kind of you.
MS. CHEN: And Mr. Russo, there's one more thing, and it's off the record again.
TOMMY: Sure, go ahead.
MS. CHEN: My father had a restaurant in Sunset Park for twenty-five years. He lost the lease in twenty nineteen.
TOMMY: I'm sorry to hear that.
MS. CHEN: He said the hardest part wasn't the money. It was that he had to fight alone. Please don't fight alone.
TOMMY: I'm not alone. I have a whole building behind me, and a daughter with a folder.
MS. CHEN: Then you're already ahead of my father. Good luck, and I mean that.
TOMMY: Thank you. Go Mets.
MS. CHEN: Go Yankees. Have a good day, both of you.

[Scene: 10:40 a.m. Court Street. Tommy and Gina stand outside the bank.]

TOMMY: Well. That's it. The bank says no.
GINA: Actually, the bank says "not at this time."
TOMMY: That's a no with a tie. A no in a suit.
GINA: Dad, she gave us a name, a nonprofit lender. That's not nothing.
TOMMY: I feel stupid. I've been open twenty years, and I can't even get a loan for a fryer.
GINA: You're not stupid. The system is backwards, and she said it herself: "circular."
TOMMY: I put eleven thousand on a credit card for a floor that isn't even mine. Now my credit is bad because of it.
GINA: That's because of Richie, and because of Harborline. It's not because of you.
TOMMY: Your mother would know what to do right now.
GINA: Mom would say, "Tommy, eat something. Then we'll talk."
TOMMY: Ha, that's true. That's exactly what she'd say.
GINA: So let's eat something, and then we'll call the nonprofit.
TOMMY: And if they say no too?
GINA: Then we try something else, and then something else again. It's like Marisol says: records, rules, and one more letter.
TOMMY: You're your mother's daughter, you know that? Stubborn.
GINA: I'm your daughter too, which means I'm double stubborn.
TOMMY: Gina, can I tell you something? I'm tired. I'm really tired.
GINA: I know, Dad. I can see it.
TOMMY: For twenty years, I thought, "If I work hard, it will be okay." I worked hard. It's not okay.
GINA: Working hard isn't enough anymore, at least not alone. Ms. Chen's father learned that the hard way.
TOMMY: She's a good person, though. For a Yankees fan.
GINA: Ha! There he is. That's my dad.
TOMMY: So what's the name of this nonprofit?
GINA: She wrote it here: "Brooklyn Neighborhood Fund," with a phone number and her name. It says, "Tell them Linda sent you."
TOMMY: "Linda sent you." It sounds like a private club.
GINA: Everything in this city is a club. You just need to know somebody who's already inside.
TOMMY: Well, I know a lot of people, after twenty years behind a bar.
GINA: Then maybe that's your real collateral. People.
TOMMY: Try telling that to a bank.
GINA: I'll tell it to the nonprofit instead, in big letters.
TOMMY: Okay. Food first, and then the call.
GINA: Food first. So where do you want to go?
TOMMY: Not my bar. I don't want to see my fryer right now.
GINA: How about the diner on Court Street?
TOMMY: The diner. Eggs, and their coffee. Their coffee is even worse than mine, and that always makes me feel better.

## 표현 정리 (낭독하지 않음)

- **금융 어휘** — *a loan / apply for a loan / approve*(대출 / 대출을 신청하다 / 승인하다), *collateral*(담보), *secure the loan*(대출을 담보로 보장하다), *revenue*(매출), *profit*(이익), *a cash reserve*(예비 자금), *interest rate*(금리), *payments of about two thousand a month*(월 상환액), *credit score / credit report / balance / limit*(신용점수 / 신용 보고서 / 잔액 / 한도), *co-sign*(공동 서명·보증하다), *guarantee*(보증하다).
- **SBA 7(a)** — 미국 중소기업청이 은행 대출의 일부를 보증해 주는 대표 프로그램.
- **격식 화법** — *May I be direct? / I do have some concerns, though. / Could you tell me, in your own words, …? / At this time, we're unable to approve the loan.*(거절의 공식 표현) / *That would change the picture completely.*
- **off the record** — 비공식적으로(기록에 남기지 않고).
- **circular** — 순환적인, 닭이 먼저냐 달걀이 먼저냐.
- **displacement** — (주민·상인의) 내몰림. 토미는 "쫓겨난다는 말의 고급 버전"이라고 한다.
- **I'll try not to hold it against you.** — "그걸로 당신을 미워하진 않도록 할게요." 양키스 팬이라는 말에 대한 농담.
- **That's a no with a tie. A no in a suit.** — "넥타이 맨 거절이네. 양복 입은 '노'." 완곡어를 비꼬는 토미식 표현.
- **That's not nothing.** — "그것도 아무것도 아닌 건 아니야(작지만 의미 있어)."
- **stubborn** — 고집 센.
- **실망 표현** — *Well. That's it. / I feel stupid. / I'm tired. I'm really tired. / It's not okay.*

## 낭독 포인트 (낭독하지 않음)

- **collateral** [컬**래**터럴], **revenue** [**레**버뉴], **displacement** [디스**플레**이스먼트], **circular** [**써**r큘러r].
- **seven-a** [세븐 에이], **six eighty** [식스 에이리] — 신용점수 680.
- 첸의 말투: 따뜻하지만 정확하다. 걱정을 *The first concern… / What's the second… / the third concern*으로 번호 매기듯 정리한다.
- *At this time, we're unable to approve the loan.* — 첸은 미안한 마음으로 천천히, 토미는 *At this time.*을 비꼬듯 되받는다.
- 첸의 아버지 이야기(*Please don't fight alone.*)는 목소리를 낮추고 진심으로.
- 마지막 다이너 커피 농담은 기운을 되찾은 톤으로.

토미: 하하, 그래. 온다. 똑바로 앉아.
첸: 루소 씨? 안녕하세요. 린다 첸입니다. 들어오세요.
토미: 안녕하세요, 첸 씨. 신청서 작성을 도와준 제 딸 지나예요.
